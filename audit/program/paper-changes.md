### A2. Section 10, the confirmation wall (lines 370 to 407)

1. **Opening (372).** Old: no edge of ordinary size can be confirmed "by this gate or by any valid test". New: at those lengths an edge of ordinary size cannot be confirmed by a fixed-length test that trusts a Gaussian approximation, and the gate's exact test needs several times more. Source: 2.3.
2. **The arithmetic (374).** Keep through the Bailey and López de Prado sentence. Delete "The gate's betting test pays for validity ... Only more independent observations move the answer." Add: the table is a planning reference that assumes independent, near-Gaussian observations, and a test that assumes less pays more. Source: 2.1.
3. **New paragraph after Table `tab:wall`, "The exact test and its cap".** A test that assumes only its null is a bet (Ville; Kelly; Shafer; Waudby-Smith and Ramdas). If a period's return is at least `−L` with non-positive conditional mean, wealth `Π(1 + λr)` with predictable `0 ≤ λ ≤ 1/L` reaches `1/α` with probability at most `α`. Its expected log growth at a constant bet is `λμ − λ²(σ² + μ²)/2`: at Kelly's bet `μ/σ²` that is `(μ/σ)²/2`, so wealth reaches 20 at `z² = 2 log 20 = 6.0`, the yardstick's scale, but only when `μ/σ² ≤ 1/L`. The gate has `L = 2` and four-hour volatility 2%: at Sharpe 4.9 Kelly's bet is 5.2 and the cap is 0.5. Capped, expected log wealth reaches `log 20` over 360, 546, 1,080, 2,190 and 5,400 steps only at Sharpe 39, 26, 13, 6.6 and 2.8. These are thresholds on expected log wealth, not 80% power. A binary contract bought near an even price loses at most its stake, about one standard deviation, so its cap binds far later. Source: 2.2; `capped_bet`.
4. **The cutoff bounds T (391).** Old: "the gate's bet reaches p = 0.05 in expectation only near 6.0 and 4.9. A year after the cutoff the figure is still 2.5." New: the gate's capped bet reaches it only near 39 and 26. A year after the cutoff the yardstick still asks 2.5 and the gate 6.6. Source: 2.2, 2.3.
5. **What this shows (395).** Old: "The second fact is arithmetic, not a defect of the gate ... on any venue and with any kernel." New: the second fact has two causes. The calendar, which no test removes: on the yardstick an ordinary edge takes six years. And the gate's cap, a property of that test on leveraged four-hour returns, which raises its threshold from about 5 to about 26. For a frontier model's systematic strategy a rapid and honest confirmation is therefore not available at ordinary Sharpe. Source: 2.3.
6. **What it does not show (397).** Old: "It does not show that the gate lacks power on long holdouts." New: it does not show that no exact test can do better on these returns. The gate's test needs Sharpe 3.4 even over two years, so a campaign on a longer holdout owes a re-derived confirmer first. Source: 2.2; section 3 B.
7. **How the wall moves (399 to 404).**
   - Item 1. Old: "which moves the 80% figure to about Sharpe 2". New: add "on the yardstick; the gate's test would still need about 3.4".
   - Item 2. Old: "A higher S through breadth ..." (the whole item). New: **a return whose worst case is near its typical size**, so that the exact test runs near the yardstick. Bars of a leveraged perpetual are not such a return; stakes in binary contracts are. Breadth stays in Table `tab:rates`, row D.
   - Item 3. Delete "Forecasts of exactly resolved events arrive by the hundred each month": no count has been made.
   - Source: 2.3.
8. **After the list (405).** Old: "The roadmap is built on the third. It compares designs by their information rate ...". New: the roadmap is built on the second and third. Months on the yardstick are `6.18/(d²n)` for per-observation effect `d` and `n` independent observations a month; a design judged by an exact test states its own computation. Source: section 3.
9. **The unit of an edge claim (407).** Replace the paragraph. New: overlapping bets are dependent. A weekly marked portfolio judged by a Student statistic does not survive that: its null gives the weekly returns a non-positive conditional mean and nothing else. Take 60 events a week at price 0.50, of which exactly 31 pay with probability 30/31 and none otherwise. Each event has probability one half, the weekly profit is +1 or −30 with mean zero, and no losing week occurs in 26 with probability 0.43. The cash of a self-financing account needs only the null and the fact that a stake cannot lose more than itself: against the same null it needs 92 winning weeks in a row to reach 20, which has probability 0.049. In every study that claims edge, the judged statistic is the cash of one pre-registered paper account. A Kelly bettor's log wealth against market prices is the log likelihood ratio of its forecasts against the market's. Remove the citations of `jaffe1974` and `fama1998`, and their bibliography entries if nothing else cites them. Source: 2.5; `student_fails`; GPT's finding 1.

2. **Paragraph 2.** Replace. New: the account of section 5.1 with its proof. Cash starts at 1. A purchase pays stake `s ≤` cash at all-in cost `c` per unit and holds `s/c` units; a payout `(s/c)·x` enters cash when the settlement value `x ∈ [0, 1]` is recorded. Null: `E[x | record at registration] ≤ c` for every purchase. Then cash plus the conditional expected payout of open positions is a non-negative supermartingale from 1: a purchase replaces `s` of cash with a claim worth at most `s`, and a conditional expectation is a martingale between purchases. Cash never exceeds it, so by Ville's inequality cash reaches 20 with probability at most 5%, under any dependence, missing records or stopping rule. The mirror buys the complement at `1 − c` with the same fraction of its own cash, and is a supermartingale under the hypothesis that every purchase has non-negative expected net return.
