\title{What Can Be Confirmed About an Agent's Edge?\\[0.3em]\large Auditable search, a confirmation wall, and two positive controls}
\author{Kevin Lei}
\date{Working paper, November 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Language-model agents can search large hypothesis spaces cheaply. In mathematics that search yields results because a formal checker rejects every candidate that does not follow from its definitions. In trading research the checker is a backtest, which is noisy, non-stationary and exploitable by selection. This paper asks what can and cannot be confirmed about an agent's trading edge, and builds a system to study it. Agents write strategies in a typed language that a small trusted kernel evaluates on point-in-time data; an append-only ledger records every evaluation and every result disclosed to an agent; data sources bind only under certificates; and a statistical gate separates selection on a search period from one test on untouched data, with the whole adaptive procedure calibrated on simulated markets. The language's core invariants are proved in Lean, and a second kernel, an executable Lean engine now being built, carries the proofs down to the code that runs. A pilot on Hyperliquid perpetual futures ran the system end to end. It promoted nothing under the null and nothing from planted edges of annualized Sharpe 1 to 3. The cause is arithmetic: a strategy a language model proposes can be judged only on data after the model's knowledge cutoff, so the judged span is months. At 91 days, 80\% power needs a Sharpe ratio of 5.0 for a test of fixed length, 5.9 for an exact sequential test and 27.6 for the pilot's gate; at two years the exact test needs 2.07. We call this the confirmation wall, and design the program that follows around it as two positive controls. Study~1, now running, asks whether the agent finds a planted edge more often than a genetic search of 100 evaluations, by a sequential sign test of size at most 0.043. Study~2, in construction, asks whether an exact confirmer can confirm a published rule with a live record: a long-only dip-buying vault, replicated in the Lean engine and judged over two forward years. For a long-only rule full investment is the wall, so its expected end is ``not confirmed'', and its forward run is kept as a measurement. Formal methods can make certain errors impossible; they cannot turn short evidence into confirmation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Agentic mathematics is search with a learned heuristic. Many agents propose candidate lemmas, proof steps or constructions at high throughput, and the language model plays the part of the heuristic in classical tree search: it decides which branches look promising, not which are correct. Correctness comes from elsewhere. A proof assistant such as Lean has a small trusted kernel that checks each candidate against the formal definitions, cheaply and mechanically, so wrong guesses cost almost nothing and the heuristic may be wild. Even that guarantee is narrower than it looks: the kernel certifies that a proof matches its formal statement, not that the statement captures the question. A trading evaluator has the same weakness in a stronger form, since it can compute a perfectly correct result for an incorrectly modeled market.

This paper asks how much of that structure carries over to quantitative research, and what a verifier can honestly confirm there. Three questions organize it. \emph{Capability:} when an edge exists, does an agent find it more often than cheap search? \emph{Edge:} does anything an agent proposes earn a positive expected net return, in a way that can be confirmed? \emph{Information:} does reading change an agent's forecasts for the better? The paper answers the first two as far as the calendar allows, and explains why no study of the third has honest power today (Section~\ref{sec:closed}). Its contributions come in four parts.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A system for auditable search} (Section~\ref{sec:system}). Agents propose freely, and results come only through a small trusted kernel. It type-checks strategies written in a restricted language, evaluates them on point-in-time data with explicit timing and costs, and records every evaluation and every disclosure in an append-only ledger. A statistical gate separates selection, which may use anything in a search period, from inference, one test on untouched data, and the whole adaptive procedure is calibrated on simulated markets. The system is one of the project's products, not only a means to a result.
  \item \textbf{A pilot} (Section~\ref{sec:pilot}) ran the system on Hyperliquid's perpetual futures with a frontier model as the agent. It promoted nothing under the null and nothing from planted edges, and the agent's one real campaign froze no finalist.
  \item \textbf{The confirmation wall} (Section~\ref{sec:wall}). The pilot's blindness is arithmetic. A strategy a language model proposes can be judged only on data after the model's knowledge cutoff, so the judged span is months. We give the threshold at matched power for three tests, separate the part that is the calendar from the part that is the gate's design, and state the three ways the wall moves.
  \item \textbf{Two positive controls} (Section~\ref{sec:program}), designed around the wall. Study~1 asks the capability question on synthetic markets, where observations are free, and is running. Study~2 asks whether an exact confirmer confirms a rule that plausibly has an edge, a published strategy with a live record replicated and judged forward, and is in construction. We give their designs and their pre-registered criteria; neither has a result yet.
\end{enumerate}
The project is academic. It trades nowhere, reads only public data under source certificates, and claims no edge for any strategy. Every criterion that judges a number is registered before the evidence it judges exists. Section~\ref{sec:related} relates the work to the literature, and Section~\ref{sec:limits} states its limits.

\section{Search against a noisy verifier}\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Selection is not confirmation}\label{sec:selection}

The analogy to mathematics breaks at the verifier. A proof checker is exact and cannot be fooled. A backtest is a noisy estimate on non-stationary data, and searching harder against it produces better-looking results whether or not any edge exists. A pass/fail filter does not avoid this: accepting only strategies above a Sharpe threshold is still selection. In an illustrative Gaussian example, searching a thousand null strategies and ten modest real edges accepts mostly noise (Appendix~\ref{app:noise}). Agents make this worse in three ways.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Hidden trials.} An agent that tweaks a parameter, looks at the result and tweaks again has run many tests while submitting one.
  \item \textbf{Unclear effective $N$.} A thousand near-duplicates are neither 1{,}000 independent tests nor one.
  \item \textbf{Non-stationarity.} Even a correctly validated edge shows only that the edge existed in the sample period.
\end{enumerate}
Two design goals follow. The complete history of what agents saw and decided must be auditable, so that the selection procedure can be evaluated as a whole rather than one test at a time. And inference must use only data that played no part in selection.

Short histories leave little search budget. Breadth is the second budget. Grinold's fundamental law \cite{grinold1989} says that the information ratio is roughly skill per independent bet times the square root of independent bets per period. A dozen co-moving crypto assets offer few independent bets; a larger universe may offer more, but breadth must be measured from the covariance of strategy returns, not counted as instruments. Time lowers the noise ceiling, breadth raises the signal of a real edge, and trials raise the ceiling, so a search is affordable only where the first two are large. For a strategy that a language model proposes there is a third condition: only time after the model's knowledge cutoff counts (Section~\ref{sec:wall}).

\section{A system for auditable search}\label{sec:system}

\subsection{A small trusted kernel}\label{sec:arch}

The system splits into an untrusted generator and a trusted kernel, and the only way to obtain a backtest result is through the kernel (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). Agents propose strategies, each with a stated mechanism. The kernel type-checks them, reduces them to a canonical form and logs them, evaluates them, and gates them. Every result shown to an agent, including for rejected candidates, is recorded in the ledger, because it can shape what the agent tries next.

The kernel's source should be small, but the trusted computing base is larger than the kernel. Its correctness also depends on the reconstruction and ingestion of historical data, revisions and timestamps included; on source certificates and the tests that produce them (Section~\ref{sec:sources}); on the language's compiler and runtime, and the proof checker that verifies them (Section~\ref{sec:verified}); on the market simulator with its fee and execution models; and on the statistical implementations and the ledger's storage. Each needs stated invariants and tests.

``Results come only through the kernel'' is a deployment requirement, not a property of the design. An agent that can load a file of prices and run its own backtest can do unlogged research, and nothing in the kernel would know. The deployment therefore states a threat model: the channels through which an agent could learn about data or results (files, network access, tools, error messages, caches, other agents and earlier sessions) and the control that closes each. Tool permissions alone do not close these channels, so the operating system enforces them. In the pilot a research agent runs in a sandbox whose only tool is a client for a daemon. The daemon fixes the data store, enforces the evaluation budget and discloses a fixed set of fields. The client is read-only and ignores code the agent writes, the sandbox sees only the software it runs, and its only network path is a logged relay to the model's API.

For licensed data the daemon is also the licensing boundary. Some vendors' terms permit storage and deterministic processing but restrict use with language-model systems. Such a source's rows stay behind the daemon, and agents, hosted models included, receive only aggregate reports, so the terms are met at one enforced point. Our first design of those reports was shown to leak individual values (Appendix~\ref{app:defects}, row~9), so until it is redesigned no aggregate of such a source crosses. Even with every channel closed, the ledger is an audit mechanism, not a measure of information leakage. It records what was disclosed, not how much an agent inferred from it, and it cannot see what the agent already knew from training.

\subsection{A typed strategy language}\label{sec:dsl}

Agents do not write arbitrary backtests. They write strategies in a small domain-specific language designed to rule out specified classes of research bugs before anything runs. A strategy is data, an abstract syntax tree that the kernel type-checks and interprets, never host-language code. Each property below is stated as an invariant and tested, including against strategies written to evade it, and the core ones are proved (Section~\ref{sec:verified}).

\paragraph{Causality in data and in execution.} A strategy is a causal stream transformer, formally a Mealy machine: its output at time $t$ depends only on inputs it has received. A type signature describes only an interface; the guarantee comes from the runtime, which feeds the machine only point-in-time data and enforces four distinct times: when data became available, when the strategy decided, when its order could be submitted, and when that order could fill. A strategy that reads a candle's close at 12:00 and fills at that close is causal in its inputs but still leaks through execution, and timestamps alone cannot exclude it, since such a fill can satisfy every time ordering with equality.

\begin{lstlisting}
-- A strategy consumes point-in-time observations and emits orders
type Strategy = Mealy (Obs Event) Order

-- Each observation records when it became knowable and which revision it is
data Obs a = Obs { eventTime :: Time, knownAt :: Time, revision :: Int, value :: a }

-- The simulator, not the strategy, decides submission and fill times
data Fill = Fill { decidedAt :: Time, submittedAt :: Time
                 , filledAt :: Time, price :: Price }
\end{lstlisting}

Every event therefore carries a sequence number as well as a time, and the evaluator enforces two conditions. For every input to a decision,
\begin{equation}
  t_{\mathrm{known}} \le t_{\mathrm{decision}} \le t_{\mathrm{submission}} \le t_{\mathrm{fill}},
\end{equation}
and the fill is computed only from market events strictly after the order became eligible to execute, ordered by time and then by sequence number, so that events sharing a timestamp still have a definite order. Any exception, such as an auction that crosses at a price set after submission, is modeled explicitly. In the pilot, a decision using the close at $B$ occurs at $B+\delta$ and fills at the opening reference at $B+4$ hours (Figure~\ref{fig:fill}; Appendix~\ref{app:execution}). Even a correct fill rule cannot establish that a fill would have happened: queue position, cancellations, competing orders and latency are not fully visible in historical data, so fills are an estimate from an execution model. Event-driven engines such as QuantConnect's LEAN \cite{lean} already separate a time frontier from fill models, and the language must at least match that.

\paragraph{Units, dimensions and time bases.} Prices, sizes, funding rates and volatilities carry types, so adding a price to a funding rate fails to type-check. Rates also carry their time basis: an hourly and an annualized funding rate are both dimensionless ratios, but combining them without an explicit conversion is a type error. Types do not stop a feature from being normalized with future data; that is the runtime's job.

\paragraph{Levels and changes.} Two independent trending series routinely show large, meaningless correlations \cite{granger1974}. The design therefore distinguishes levels from changes: correlation, regression and ranking operators are to accept only changes, ratios or other transforms declared stationary, unless the strategy invokes a cointegration test whose result is a logged finding rather than a license. This is not built. The pilot's language has no correlation or regression operator, and its ranking operator accepts any dimensionless value.

\paragraph{Composition.} Causal transformers compose: features feed signals, signals feed sizing, sizing feeds risk limits. Laws of composition give the canonicalizer of Section~\ref{sec:ledger} rewrites to apply. Each rewrite must preserve full trading semantics, including state, order timing and fees, and in binary64 arithmetic only identities exact bit for bit qualify (Appendix~\ref{app:rewrites}).

\paragraph{Execution realism.} Fees, funding payments, slippage and latency are part of the evaluator's semantics, not optional parameters. Each venue needs its own model: on Hyperliquid, the fee tier a small account actually pays, funding schedules and order-book depth. The pilot fits one constant cost per coin, side and size bucket to a 24-hour recording of order books, so depth at the decision time is not modeled (Appendix~\ref{app:costs}). Such models are only as good as their calibration, so they are treated as validated components with stated error bounds, and where calibration data is thin the limitation is reported beside every result that depends on it.

\subsection{Sources: declaration, certification, point in time}\label{sec:sources}

A strategy should be able to say ``this depends on a feed of shipping disruptions'' before anyone has built that feed. The language therefore treats a data source as a typed declaration, separate from any concrete feed: an entity key, typed fields with units, a cadence or event semantics, and an availability policy. The known time of every observation is assigned by the recorder or the certified backfill, never by the feed itself.

\begin{lstlisting}
source ShippingAlerts : EventStream {
  key      : Region
  kind     : Enum [Closure, Delay, Attack]
  count    : Count
}  -- knownAt is assigned by the recorder, never by the feed

strategy S (alerts : ShippingAlerts, brent : Price Commodity) = ...

bind ShippingAlerts := feed "alerts-v3"   -- refused without a valid certificate
\end{lstlisting}

The type checker checks a strategy against the declared schema without any binding. Evaluation requires every source the strategy names to be bound to a certified feed; an unbound source makes evaluation undefined, not empty. Agents can therefore propose hypotheses about data that does not yet exist, and those hypotheses wait in the ledger until a feed is certified. An unbound declaration prevents fitting through this system before the feed binds, but it does not make the strategy a pre-registered prediction: the agent may already know, from training data, published research or an equivalent feed, how the relationship behaved. Only a frozen strategy evaluated on data independent of its selection supplies independent evidence.

\paragraph{Certification.} A feed binds to a declaration only under a certificate recorded in the ledger. The certificate holds the results of tests whose thresholds are fixed before the feed's data is examined against any returns.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Terms:} the certificate quotes the clause that permits the automated use, with its location and retrieval date, or shows that the feed is an official API whose terms allow it. Silence is not permission. A source whose terms forbid automated access is never fetched, and anything collected from it before that was found is deleted. Where the terms restrict the operator rather than the access, as some venues' interface terms restrict residents of certain jurisdictions, reading the public data is the operator's decision, recorded with the clauses found.
  \item \textbf{Availability:} every historical timestamp is classified as observed (recorded live, or witnessed by an independent archive capture or a protocol-enforced time), reconstructed from the source's own release records, or estimated. A lag measured on a live recording validates the availability rule going forward; applied to history it is an assumption, since lags change. A historical row's known time is therefore the later of the claimed-time rule, used only where the claimed time is protocol-enforced or server-set and the live test passed, and any independent witness. A row with neither is usable only prospectively.
  \item \textbf{Revisions:} a later re-fetch of the same history, compared against what was recorded live, so that a backtest never sees corrected values nobody had at the time.
  \item \textbf{Entity mapping:} the precision and recall of the mapping from raw records to schema keys, measured on blind-labelled samples.
  \item \textbf{Coverage:} gaps, outages, and the period over which the feed is complete.
  \item \textbf{Extractor validation} for feeds derived by a language model (below).
\end{itemize}
Agents may build feeds, but the kernel's certification tests decide whether a feed binds, so an agent never grades its own data.

\paragraph{The point-in-time store.} A known time is necessary but not sufficient: real data is revised, backfilled, delayed by ingestion and sometimes mistimestamped. The store keeps immutable revisions with lineage. A query ``as of time $t$'' resolves each key to the latest revision fetched by the dataset version and shows it only if its known time is at most $t$. It never falls back to an older revision, so a later correction hides a value rather than replacing it with one nobody had, and the revisions test measures what that costs. A revision stores only what was observed: the values, the source's own timestamps, and when it was fetched. Its known time is computed by the availability rule that the dataset version pins, so a corrected rule reaches every revision at once. Some public archives already work this way: ALFRED \cite{alfred} serves U.S.\ macroeconomic series as each vintage was published, dated by day, so it establishes which value was current on a date but not when it became tradable within that day.

\paragraph{Sources extracted by a language model.} When a language model turns raw text into structured events, it is restricted to factual fields: who, what kind of event, and when. Judgments such as sentiment or importance are excluded, because a model that knows how events turned out can shade them with hindsight. The extractor is validated with names and dates masked, its outputs are cached with the model version and prompt hash, and that version is part of every experiment's identity. Feed design choices, such as which keywords define an event, are themselves forking paths, so a feed's specification is registered before any return conditioned on it is examined.

\paragraph{Acquisition.} Free and official sources come first, and adding a feed is a declaration, a terms record and a certificate, never a code change. Data read from a blockchain's own node or official archive carries no data vendor's terms, though the access route has terms and costs of its own. Where a source keeps no past versions, our own recording is its only point-in-time record, so recording starts early and is kept in more than one copy. A schema with many sources also makes it easy to generate plausible paths from one source to another, from a central bank's statement to the currencies it affects to the commodities they price, and each such path is one more trial (Section~\ref{sec:budget}).

\subsection{The experiment ledger}\label{sec:ledger}

The ledger makes the research process auditable; it does not by itself produce a trial count that can be plugged into a correction. A canonical strategy is a program, while a statistical trial is about information: what an agent saw, and how that shaped what it selected next. An agent that inspects one strategy's drawdown, yearly returns and cost sensitivity and then proposes something new has spent multiplicity without creating any new canonical form. Conversely, rerunning an identical deterministic backtest, or rejecting a strategy at the type checker before it touches data, spends none. The ledger's storage rejects every update and deletion, and it records six things, kept distinct:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Strategy identity:} the canonical form, so that equivalent programs are recognized.
  \item \textbf{Experiment identity:} strategy, parameters, universe, data window, dataset and certificate versions, availability conventions, execution and fee model versions, extractor versions, simulator version and random seeds.
  \item \textbf{Disclosures:} every statistic returned to an agent, including for rejected candidates.
  \item \textbf{Selection decisions:} which candidates an agent or the gate chose to pursue, and what it had seen at that point.
  \item \textbf{Source certificates:} the tests and thresholds under which each feed was bound.
  \item \textbf{Registered forward predictions,} with the documents read before each.
\end{enumerate}
A result is committed to the ledger before anything about it is shown, so a failed write cannot leave unlogged research. Multiplicity is then estimated from this history, and the estimates are checked by running the whole procedure on markets with no edge (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).

\paragraph{Canonical forms.} Strategies are rewritten with the language's algebraic laws, for example commuting independent features or folding constant transforms. Equality saturation with e-graphs \cite{willsey2021} finds expressions provably equal under the supplied rules and extracts one by a cost model. It does not produce a unique form for every semantically equivalent program, and its results depend on rule coverage and resource limits. The claim is therefore narrow: the system detects some provable equivalences, conditional on the soundness of its rules, which Section~\ref{sec:verified} proves.

\paragraph{Effective $N$.} Strategies that differ syntactically can still behave almost identically. Clustering return streams by correlation gives one estimate of the effective number of independent trials, in the spirit of Bailey and L\'opez de Prado, but it is a heuristic: the right adjustment depends on the joint distribution of the test statistics, and the correlations are estimated on the sample being searched. The gate therefore counts trials raw, reports how its decisions change at the cluster counts, and treats a promotion that holds only under a favorable count as fragile.

\paragraph{Search guidance.} Agents can ask which regions of strategy space are already explored, which steers them toward new hypotheses. Telling agents where past results were good or bad is a disclosure, logged and accounted for like any other.

\subsection{The statistical gate}\label{sec:gate}

Individually valid tests do not make a valid combined procedure. The gate therefore separates selection from inference. Everything computed on the search period, the screen, Hansen's SPA, the Romano--Wolf stepdown and the mechanism check, is a selection rule: it decides which candidates reach the holdout, and its behavior is calibrated by simulation, but it supports no error claim of its own. Romano--Wolf's guarantees, for example, do not survive applying it to a family that was itself chosen on the same data. The only inference is the holdout batch: finalists frozen without any holdout data are tested once on that untouched period, with Holm's correction across them. Each campaign runs a gate fixed before it starts, and its error rate is claimed only for its finalist family on its holdout, under the holdout test's scope assumption that every net return before the venue absorbs a shortfall is at least $-2$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Search period only.} Agents see results from a research period. The holdout is never shown to them.
  \item \textbf{Screen.} A candidate must reach a deflated Sharpe ratio of 0.95, with $N$ the count of distinct canonical strategies evaluated that filled at least once (Appendix~\ref{app:dsr}). Recomputed at the counts of correlation clusters at $|\rho|\ge0.9$ and $0.5$, the screen is reported as a diagnostic of fragility. (Known defect, Table~\ref{tab:defects}, row~1.)
  \item \textbf{Global screen.} Hansen's SPA runs over every strategy evaluated in the campaign against a flat position. If it does not reject at 5\%, the campaign promotes nothing, which under the complete null stops most campaigns before any holdout is spent. (Row~1.)
  \item \textbf{Ranking.} The Romano--Wolf stepdown at 5\% runs over the screened candidates, and those it rejects become finalists. Its $p$-values serve selection only, since the family was chosen on the same data. (Row~1.)
  \item \textbf{Mechanism check.} A candidate whose pre-registered mechanism prediction fails is removed from the finalists. There is no override by a higher statistical threshold. (Row~5.)
  \item \textbf{Freeze.} The finalists, the promotion rule and the correction across finalists are fixed before the holdout is opened.
  \item \textbf{Holdout batch.} All finalists are evaluated together, once. The kernel enforces this: it reads the whole batch before evaluating any of it, accepts it only if its trials are exactly the finalists frozen in the ledger, and claims the window under a lock before the first evaluation. A finalist is promoted only if its net holdout return is positive and its holdout $p$-value passes 5\% after Holm's correction, and a promotion is then confirmed on a later forward window. (Row~2.)
  \item \textbf{No recycling.} A strategy modified after its holdout result is not retested on that holdout. A holdout used by one campaign is spent: the next campaign evaluates on a later period, and the old holdout becomes part of its search data.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The holdout test.} For each finalist $i$, the null hypothesis is that at every step of the holdout its net return, before the venue absorbs any shortfall, has non-positive mean given everything before that step, which includes everything the search revealed:
\begin{equation}
  H_{0,i}:\ \E\big[r^{\mathrm{net}}_{i,t} \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big] \le 0 \quad \text{for every } t, \qquad \mathcal{F}_{t-1} \supseteq \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{search}} .
\end{equation}
The test bets: it stakes a predictable, capped plug-in Kelly fraction of its wealth on each step's return and takes the inverse of the wealth's running maximum as a $p$-value \cite{ville1939,shafer2021}. Under a lower bound of $-2$ on pre-absorption returns this is valid under any dependence and any stopping, and Holm's correction then bounds any false promotion in the frozen family at 5\%, bankrupt paths included (Appendix~\ref{app:bet}). That bound is proved only for the corrected synthetic construction (Appendix~\ref{app:loss}), not for the frozen pilot's engine or for real markets, and the reported floor of $-1$ is not a substitute. A locked suite of null series checks the implementation, not the theorem's premises in every market. A rejection certifies a positive conditional mean at some step, not a positive unconditional mean of the unscaled strategy. (Rows~3 and~6.) The cap on the bet follows from the loss bound and costs power (Section~\ref{sec:wall}): at the pilot's holdout lengths zero promotions is not evidence of no edge.

\paragraph{Calibration and scale.} Cheap baseline searchers supply large samples of campaigns under the null and under planted edges. Expensive agent campaigns are smoke tests, with much weaker bounds (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}).

\paragraph{Across campaigns.} Error control is per campaign, and every promotion is reported beside the number of campaigns run. A project-wide claim needs a summable error budget fixed in advance (Appendix~\ref{app:bet}); alpha-investing \cite{foster2008} controls a different quantity and is not a substitute.

\paragraph{Reported, not judged.} Beside each finalist's judged result the gate reports its holdout return at twice the modeled costs, and the share of the holdout whose market conditions fall within the range the cost model was calibrated on. These appear only after the promotion decision and never choose among finalists. They exist because cost models calibrated on short windows may understate costs exactly when markets move and strategies trade most.

The deflated Sharpe ratio is a selection heuristic, not inference after adaptive search (Appendix~\ref{app:dsr}). Every answer from a holdout leaks some information, and what matters is how much each answer reveals, not only how many there are. The reusable holdout of Dwork et al.\ \cite{dwork2015} releases answers through a noisy threshold, with guarantees under adaptive queries for independent samples. The gate instead spends each holdout once, in one batch, and claims no adaptive validity beyond that.

\paragraph{Mechanism obligations.} Every strategy ships with a stated mechanism that implies tests beyond profit. It names the counterparty and why they trade at this price; they need not be wrong, since forced sellers at liquidation, hedgers paying for immediacy and index funds rebalancing on a schedule can all be acting rationally. It states conditional predictions, with tolerances, of how the effect should vary if the mechanism is real: a liquidation-cascade strategy should earn more when positions are crowded near liquidation and less when funding has normalized. And it estimates capacity and decay. Predictions are registered before they are tested and are logged like any other experiment. A failed prediction is evidence that the stated explanation is wrong, not proof that the strategy is, so it removes the candidate from that campaign's finalists, and the candidate may return with a revised mechanism in a later campaign on fresh data. Checked on the selection sample, as in the pilot, the test is weak (row~5). The working hypothesis that language models are most useful for proposing economically plausible ideas, rather than for blind search, is tested against simpler generators through the same machinery (Section~\ref{sec:study1}).

\subsection{Verified kernels}\label{sec:verified}

The language's semantics, type checker and canonicalization rules are the parts of the kernel whose correctness can be proved rather than only tested. A proof checker certifies that a proof matches its statement, not that the statement says what was intended, and an agent that writes both a law and its proof can state a weak law and prove it. Laws are therefore treated like pre-registered criteria (Section~\ref{sec:process}): written first, reviewed by a model from another lab, frozen, and never weakened. A law found wrong gets a dated revision, and proofs of the old statement do not count toward the new one.

\paragraph{The reference kernel.} The pilot's production engine is written in Rust. A Lean~4 \cite{demoura2021} model of its language and simulator serves as a reference, in which six theorems are proved.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Non-interference.} For every well-typed strategy $s$, two input histories that agree on every observation known by time $t$ produce the same decision at $t$:
  \begin{equation}
    h_1|_{\mathrm{known} \le t} = h_2|_{\mathrm{known} \le t} \implies \mathrm{decide}_s(h_1, t) = \mathrm{decide}_s(h_2, t).
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Unit soundness.} Every well-typed expression evaluates to a value of its declared unit and time basis.
  \item \textbf{Rewrite soundness.} Each canonicalization rule preserves the strategy's full trading semantics, including state and order timing.
  \item \textbf{Fill ordering.} Every fill satisfies both timing conditions of Section~\ref{sec:dsl}.
  \item \textbf{Binding.} Evaluation is undefined for any strategy that names an unbound source.
  \item \textbf{Bounded returns.} Losses are capped at account equity, so every reported per-step return is at least $-1$.
\end{enumerate}
Numbers are abstract in the proofs, since several algebraic identities, associativity of addition among them, are false in binary64. The canonicalizer therefore admits only rewrites that are exact in binary64, and the reference and the production engine are compared bit for bit, on complete node streams, over a frozen corpus of programs and markets. A second candidate reference, in another language, was held to the same criteria and removed (Section~\ref{sec:pilot-sources}).

\paragraph{What proofs reach.} Proofs establish properties of a formal semantics, the first of three levels of assurance (Table~\ref{tab:assurance}). A simulator can satisfy its fill-ordering theorem while charging the wrong fee schedule or misjudging liquidity, and two implementations that agree can share one wrong model of the market.

\paragraph{Kernel version 2.} The pilot's engine evaluates a fixed four-hour grid of bars under a constant cost table. Replaying a real account's orders needs more: limit and maker orders at any timescale, fills against recorded order books and trades, and an exact account. The second kernel is a clean-room design for that, written as an executable Lean program. Everything that decides a number is a pure Lean function: the strategy parser and checker, both evaluators, order handling, the fill rules, the ledger with its fees, funding, margin and liquidation, the reading of recorded decimals, the report, and the confirmer of study~2. OCaml carries only data loading, input and output, and orchestration. The laws are theorems about the functions that run, stated and frozen before their proofs and resting only on Lean's three standard axioms. Where a function is too slow, a faster twin is proved equal to it and substituted by the compiler, so no law speaks of code that does not run.

Four features of its semantics matter for what follows. The account is exact: prices, sizes, money and rates are rationals, and nothing in the ledger rounds. Strategy expressions stay in binary64, with IEEE~754's correctly rounded arithmetic, and the engine computes $\exp$ and $\log$ itself from exact series, returning the binary64 nearest the true value or, where its interval cannot decide, an undefined value rather than a wrong rounding; every float is therefore the same on every host. Time is a stream of recorded events (listings, funding settlements, trades, book snapshots and observations of declared sources), each with the time it happened and the time it became known; a coin with no fresh book snapshot for a registered length is stale, and nothing of it fills. Fills follow one of two named rules. The \emph{pessimistic} rule credits a resting buy only on a later recorded seller-initiated trade below its price, in that trade's size; the \emph{optimistic} rule fills it whenever the record does not exclude it. Neither bounds the other, and judged runs use the pessimistic rule. Finally, no fill beats the tape: every fill is at a price no better than the coin's price after its event, and no fee is negative, so a trade never raises an account's value at the moment it happens. Study~2's validity rests on that law (Section~\ref{sec:study2}).

Bars remain available as a derived source: a bar becomes two trades at its extremes and a one-level quote at its close, under four registered assumptions about the spread, the depth, the path and whether the extremes traded. The engine refuses any event dated in a protected window until that window's release is committed, so no report or trace can hold protected data. A run's identity hashes its program, every input file to the length read, the venue model and the content hash of the build that made the executable, and nothing is published before its result is committed to the ledger. The two kernels share one component, the float expression language, and version~2's evaluator is checked bit for bit against the pilot's Lean reference rebuilt with correctly rounded $\exp$ and $\log$ and a numerically stable rule for rolling windows. When study~1 ends the pilot's engine is retired; its judged results stay reproducible from an archived build, and none is rerun on the new one. Version~2 is being built, and the parts complete so far have their laws proved.

\subsection{Pre-registration and independent checks}\label{sec:process}

Statistical procedures fail most often where someone can choose after seeing a number. Every condition that judges a number someone could game is registered before the evidence it judges exists: measurable, with thresholds, and with each number derived from the claim it supports or stated as a choice with its reason. Before registration, reviewers try to construct a run that passes while the claim is false and one that fails while it holds. A registered criterion is never edited. A wrong one gets a dated revision, and the work is judged only on evidence produced after it. After a failure a criterion may be amended only if the change cannot increase false discoveries; the amendment is labelled as such and never reported as a pass of the original. A defect in the procedure itself that a failure exposes is fixed only as a new version judged on fresh evidence, and the failure stays recorded. Operational readiness is not pre-registered: it is a test suite.

Correctness is checked by someone other than the builder. Tests are written from the specification by a session that has not read the code, and by a model from another lab where a judged result depends on them. A defect starts as a failing test, and failure paths are tested by killing the process. A red team that has not seen the source attacks each kernel milestone, and each of its successes becomes a permanent test. Before a phase ends, a fresh session hunts for bugs in the code it rests on, and independent implementations of the statistics, written from the specification by another lab's model, must agree with ours.

Only the gate's holdout step reads a holdout, once per campaign. Nothing derived from an unreleased holdout or a reserved forward window reaches research, selection or anyone reviewing work not yet frozen. Such recordings are inspected only for integrity (their status, sizes, hashes and gaps), never for prices or returns.

\section{The pilot}\label{sec:pilot}

The pilot built the system and ran it end to end, on one venue and one family of strategies, to learn whether a strategy an agent proposes can be confirmed. This section reports what it found. It makes no trading claim, and it uses no unreleased holdout and no observation from a reserved forward window.

\subsection{Design}\label{sec:pilot-design}

The venue is Hyperliquid, a decentralized exchange whose perpetual futures, funding settlements, candles and order books are public. The family is funding dislocations, chosen because settled funding, premiums and bars have a public accounting link, unlike predicted funding, so a replay can be checked against the venue's own records. The universe is the twelve coins with the most volume, ranked in the month before each date and including coins later delisted, over a 730-day window ending 30 September 2026 whose last 180 days are held out. Features update hourly and decisions are made every four hours. The simulated account starts with \$10{,}000, targets a gross exposure of two, caps an order at \$10{,}000, and pays a constant taker cost per coin, side and size bucket (\$1{,}000 or \$10{,}000) fitted to a 24-hour recording of order books (Appendix~\ref{app:execution}). A fixed witness strategy, which takes a small position against large funding rates, tests the replay end to end; it is a fixture, not an optimized edge.

The agent is Claude Opus 5.5 in a frozen harness: the sandbox of Section~\ref{sec:arch}, a fixed brief, a budget of 100 evaluations, at most 200 turns and two hours. Type checks are free, and every evaluation call before the budget is spent counts against it, rejections and duplicates included. Three cheap searchers explore the same language through the same kernel and ledger: random-plus-genetic search with 100 or 400 evaluations, which we call G100 and G400, and a hill climber with 400. Each is pinned, together with the statistics disclosed to it.

Two judged periods follow the model's knowledge cutoff of June 2026. The agent's historical holdout runs from 1 August to 30 September 2026 (60 days, 360 four-hour steps), a month after the cutoff. A forward window from 1 November 2026 to 31 January 2027 (91 days, 546 steps) was reserved to confirm anything the historical run promoted. The cost table was fitted in October, after the historical holdout, so the historical run validates the pipeline rather than testing on untouched data (Appendix~\ref{app:data}). The pilot ran in seven phases (feasibility, the kernel, calibration on synthetic markets, a single agent, a swarm of agents, text sources, and a verified reference kernel), and each phase's exit criteria were registered before its evidence existed.

\subsection{Feasibility and the kernel}\label{sec:pilot-kernel}

The venue's history could be replayed point in time. Funding rows and bars were complete within the registered limits; the availability rule, under which a funding row is known at the later of 120 seconds after its hour and 60 seconds after its reported settlement, held on a 24-hour live recording; and the witness's replay reconciled its equity with every trade, fee and funding payment.

The execution cost model was the weak point. It met its coverage target, a model cost at or above the observed cost of walking the book in at least 90\% of held-out snapshots, in 41 of 44 cells. Three fell short: BTC sells at \$1{,}000 (0.894), SUI sells at \$10{,}000 (0.882) and kPEPE sells at \$10{,}000 (0.817). We accepted the phase with that failure recorded, never as a pass, and every later result that uses the cost table carries it. An earlier recording window had already been spent: its clock instrument measured the wrong quantity, and seven cells exceeded the limit on how far the model may overstate cost. The amendments that followed do not erase it. An attempt to replace the live table with one fitted to archived order books before the historical holdout also failed. About 30\% of snapshots matched within one second, against a required 50\%, and 32 of 44 cells missed 95\% agreement on cost, the worst at 71.9\%. The historical run therefore kept the costs fitted after its holdout.

The kernel met its invariants. For each invariant at least one deliberately planted defect was written, and the checks caught all seventeen. Adversarial programs and hand-computed fixtures passed, an independent timing checker written by another lab's model from the specification alone agreed with the engine, the witness's replay matched, and the evaluator's median time was 14.9\,ms. One check failed for an instructive reason. A canary market with a planted lookahead leak was first built with zero-mean log returns, which gives prices an arithmetic drift; the fix was a market whose prices are a martingale, not a looser alarm. The canary detects only gross lookahead (Appendix~\ref{app:kernel}).

\subsection{Calibration on synthetic markets}\label{sec:calibration}

Synthetic markets are built so that no strategy has an edge. Each bar's close is set from its open, its funding and a fresh, conditionally symmetric innovation, so that any position fixed at the bar's open earns zero expected gross profit whatever it reads (Appendix~\ref{app:nulls}). Three generator families span simple, persistent, heavy-tailed and regime-dependent volatility: Gaussian innovations; GARCH volatility with a common factor and heavy-tailed innovations, fitted to the search data; and two-state regime switching with symmetric jumps. Planted markets give two of the twelve coins a drift set by a funding-reversal function of their last funding rate, scaled so that an oracle program earns an annualized net Sharpe of 1, 2 or 3.

In the first round the screen's benchmark was the observed spread of the trials' Sharpe ratios. A real edge widens that spread, since its variants of every sign and strength are trials too, so the edge raised its own hurdle: nothing was screened in any planted cell, although SPA rejected in 94 of 98 completed campaigns of the longest cell. We re-derived the benchmark from the estimator's null sampling scale, $1/\sqrt{T-1}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:dsr}). Another lab's model implemented the new screen from its specification and agreed to $10^{-9}$, and the calibration was rerun with fresh seeds, so that the new version was judged only on new evidence and the failure stays recorded.

In the second round, under the complete null, no cell promoted anything: none of 300 campaigns in each, a 95\% upper bound of 0.0099 on the rate. In every planted Sharpe cell the false discovery rate was zero; because a campaign that promotes nothing counts as zero, this shows that few campaigns promoted, not that a promotion is reliable (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}). The sensitivity target, a true promotion in at least 30\% of planted markets, failed as written and as amended. At a planted Sharpe of 3 with the GARCH generator, 76 of 100 campaigns froze finalists and none promoted; in a cell with a 900-day history and a far larger planted mean, 5 of 100 promoted. Finalists reached the holdout in every planted cell, and in the Sharpe cells the test confirmed none of them. At 1{,}080 steps its capped bet needs a mean near 0.55\% a step, while a Sharpe of 1 to 3 is a mean of 0.011\% to 0.13\% at per-step volatility from 0.5\% to 2\%. We waived the target and recorded the failure; it was never passed. Section~\ref{sec:wall} shows how little a test of that length could have done better, and which part of the shortfall is the gate's own.

The holdout test was calibrated separately, on locked suites of null series that no one had used to design it. Its first version, a studentized block bootstrap, failed in the sparse cells of 540 and 546 steps: rejection rates of 0.0595 and 0.0602 at a nominal 5\%, with upper bounds of 0.0648 and 0.0656 above the tolerance of 0.0625. An expanded calibration still failed on rare losses, since calibrating familiar shapes does not certify arbitrary means. We replaced it with the betting test of Section~\ref{sec:gate}, and its fresh locked suite passed all 99 judged combinations of null generator, length and level, each on 20{,}000 series. That checks the implementation on bounded conditional-null series, and it certifies the suite's tolerances, not the nominal levels (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}).

\subsection{Agents in the loop}\label{sec:pilot-agents}

Twenty campaigns on simulated no-edge markets tested the single agent, in three versions. In the first, all twenty crashed before the model ran, because the job's environment lacked the funding inputs. In the second the sandbox denied the agent's writes to its workspace, so it made no evaluations and established no procedure. Under repaired permissions the third promoted nothing in twenty campaigns, each of which evaluated: the agent spent 6 to 22 of its 100 evaluations, with a median of 10.5, and stopped on its own every time.

The real campaign ran the agent and G100 on the venue's history with the holdout reserved. The agent made 9 evaluations and stopped; G100 made 100. Neither arm froze a finalist, so the holdout was never opened, the release path was not exercised, and the window stays unread. The run validates the pipeline. It is evidence neither of an edge nor of its absence, and it says nothing about which searcher is better.

A swarm of four agents shared a history and a budget of 400 evaluations, at most 100 each. Its first version required at least 200 evaluations per campaign, and its first two campaigns made 33 and 36: the floor mistook agents that stopped on their own for broken machinery. In the second version, agents in two of ten campaigns invoked the evaluation client by a path the sandbox did not permit, received no evaluation and stopped, and one campaign exposed a crash in reporting a missing log. The third passed as written: ten campaigns, none promoted, and one left unresolved because an agent attempted a tool the client refused, with no breach. No promotion in ten bounds the third version's rate at 26\% (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}). Over the three versions 22 campaigns started and 21 are known non-promotions, but the versions were redesigned in turn and are not one procedure sampled 22 times, so no pooled bound is stated. No campaign had a finalist, so the holdout and the betting test never ran: the swarm's code paths are validated, not its error control or its power. Its canonicalizer passed: every one of its 16 rewrite rules preserved every shared node's stream bit for bit on 1{,}000 random programs, and 1{,}000 rewritten pairs each reduced to one key.

The agent was not always the model its harness requested. The harness asks for Claude Opus 5.5, and when the provider's safeguards flagged a session the client fell back to Claude Opus 4.8. Of the pilot's 169 agent sessions, 60 were answered by Opus 5.5 throughout, 106 by Opus 4.8 throughout, and 3 by one response from Opus 5.5 and the rest from Opus 4.8. No judged number changes, since nothing was promoted and the null bounds hold whatever model searched. Study~1 takes the agent as it is served (Section~\ref{sec:study1}).

\subsection{Text sources and the reference kernel}\label{sec:pilot-sources}

The data phase added text sources: protocol forums' posts and a governance hub's proposals. Both were certified for terms, entity mapping and lineage, but their historical timestamps are claimed by clients and could not be validated, so both bound as prospective-only, usable for what we record ourselves. A third category, token-unlock schedules, had no free source and stayed blocked. The predecessor hash of one data migration was never recorded, and the calibration of event datasets is deferred until a search store binds one.

The verified reference phase built two candidates, in Lean and in Bend. Lean proved all six laws of Section~\ref{sec:verified} under their stated assumptions; three of them (non-interference, rewrite soundness and fill ordering) were false as first frozen and were revised, with the revisions recorded, before their proofs. The Lean reference and the production engine agreed exactly on 1{,}196 type-check verdicts and on 3{,}372 runs, compared as complete node streams. Earlier agreeing runs did not count: first the corpus was incomplete, then the runs compared digests instead of streams. Bend's binding verdict succeeded but was never a reviewed frozen law; Bend had no binary64 arithmetic and its one-coin run exhausted memory, so it could not be compared with the engine, and it was removed. The production engine was faster, 16.0\,ms against 632.9\,ms on the witness's replay, and Lean was adopted for its tooling and maintenance, not for speed.

\subsection{What independent checks found}\label{sec:pilot-checks}

Fresh sessions hunting for bugs found nine reproduced defects in the frozen engine, the ledger and the mechanism check: delisting that depended on the future, reopening after a stop-out, unbudgeted holds, sizing on equity before fills, bankrupt accounts that revived, misreported boundary fills, omitted closing costs, a mechanism check that counted exposures on which its prediction was undefined, and ledger inserts that could replace rows. They remain qualifications of the frozen pilot, and they show that its proofs do not cover its implementation. Their corrected definitions are specified for the next engine. Hunts of the recorders and plumbing found persistence and isolation failures, since fixed, which show why failure paths are tested by killing processes; they do not show that recording is complete. One recorder's request budget, set from the venue's documented rate limit, could not cover every coin's polls at a funding boundary, so its completeness target was unattainable as configured, and its recordings are kept with that qualification.

The pilot's results reproduced across machines once one binary was shipped to every host, after an early comparison differed in binary identities but not in returns, and again after a large deletion of code. An outside review of this paper's mathematics raised fourteen findings. Thirteen hold in substance and one in part; none flips a recorded judgment. Those that concern machinery no current study uses are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:defects}; the others are corrected in the text.

Taken together, the pilot found that the pipeline promotes nothing on markets without an edge, with or without agents in the loop; that at its holdout lengths it cannot see planted edges of ordinary size; and that the agent's one real campaign produced nothing to test. The next section shows that the second finding is arithmetic, not a defect to be engineered away.

\section{The confirmation wall}\label{sec:wall}

This section answers the edge question for the pilot, and its answer is about the method, not about any market: at the lengths a frontier model allows, an edge of ordinary size cannot be confirmed. The threshold depends on the test, so we state it for three tests at matched power (Table~\ref{tab:wallmatch}).

\subsection{The yardstick}

A strategy of annualized Sharpe ratio $S$ observed for $T$ years gives evidence $z=S\sqrt{T}$. The number of decisions a year cancels, since more periods mean a smaller effect in each. A one-sided 5\% test with 80\% power needs $z=1.645+0.842=2.49$, so $T=6.18/S^2$ years (Table~\ref{tab:wall}); even odds need $z=1.645$. This is the minimum track record length of Bailey and L\'opez de Prado \cite{bailey2012}. It assumes independent, near-Gaussian observations and a fixed length, and is a planning reference, not a test.

\subsection{What an exact test costs}\label{sec:exact}

A test that assumes only its null is a bet \cite{ville1939,kelly1956,shafer2021,waudbysmith2024}. If each return is at least $-L$ and has non-positive conditional mean, wealth bet at a predictable fraction of at most $1/L$ is a non-negative supermartingale, and it reaches 20 with probability at most 5\% under any dependence and any stopping rule. For independent even bets of net edge $e$ staked at Kelly's fraction, log wealth is near a Brownian motion with drift $e^2/2$ and variance $e^2$ a bet. It reaches $\log 20$ before $N$ bets with probability 0.80 at $N=8.59/e^2$, which is 1.39 times the yardstick's 6.18 (Appendix~\ref{app:confirmer}). Two properties of exact tests decide what follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The fraction is registered before the data.} A bet sized for Sharpe 2 has power 0.77 within two years at a true Sharpe of 2, 0.47 at 1.4 and 0.90 at 2.4. Averaging the bets sized for 1, 2 and 3 is worse at each (0.71, 0.41 and 0.86).
  \item \textbf{The cap is the rule's own loss bound.} Kelly's fraction fits under $1/L$ while a step's worst loss is at most $\sqrt{2190}/S$ standard deviations of a four-hour step: 23.4 at Sharpe 2. The pilot's gate has $L=2$, a hundred standard deviations of a 2\% step, which is why it needs so much more. A bound must be a property of the tested rule, since clipping a loss afterwards raises the mean and breaks the null.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The knowledge cutoff bounds the judged span}\label{sec:cutoff}

A language model's proposals are selected on everything it read before its knowledge cutoff: prices, and a literature fitted to those prices. That is a search over history that no ledger records and no null market detects, so only data after the cutoff, plus a margin, can judge what the model proposes. Cutoffs are imprecise and later fine-tuning can leak later information, so the margin should be generous. Research on forecasting with language models evaluates on questions resolved after the cutoff for the same reason \cite{halawi2024,karger2025}. A frontier model is recent by definition, so its judged span is months. The pilot's model has a June 2026 cutoff, a historical holdout of 60 days (360 steps) and a forward window of 91 days (546 steps). At those lengths a test of fixed length has 80\% power only at a Sharpe of 6.1 and 5.0; at 91 days an exact test needs 5.87 and the gate 27.6. A year after the cutoff the three figures are 2.49, 2.93 and 7.4.

\subsection{What the pilot shows, and what it does not}

\paragraph{What it observed.} Planted edges of Sharpe 1 to 3 were never promoted, though their finalists reached the holdout (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}), and the agent's real campaign froze no finalist. Under the null the pipeline was clean: no promotion in 300 campaigns per calibration cell, none in the 20 judged single-agent campaigns, and 21 known non-promotions among 22 swarm campaigns.

\paragraph{What this shows.} The pipeline promotes nothing on markets without an edge, and at these lengths it cannot see an ordinary edge either. The second fact has two causes. One is the calendar, which no test removes. The other is the gate's loss cap, a choice of its test, which raises the threshold at 91 days from about 6 to about 28. The pilot's sensitivity failure is both together. For a systematic strategy proposed by a frontier model, a rapid and honest confirmation is not available at ordinary Sharpe.

\paragraph{What it does not show.} It does not show that no edge exists, or that agents cannot find one: the agent's one real campaign made 9 evaluations. It does not show that no exact test does better than the gate: one with the rule's own loss bound needs 2.07 at two years where the gate needs 4.0. And it says nothing about questions whose observations are not return periods. The pilot's failures are consistent with the wall; the wall itself is derived, not sampled.

\subsection{How the wall moves}\label{sec:moves}

The wall moves only by more independent observations, in three ways.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A longer calendar.} Wait, or use a model whose cutoff is old enough that its post-cutoff history already exists. The models a subscription still serves are at most about two years old, which moves the 80\% figure to about Sharpe 2. The designer's own hindsight then enters through the brief and the universe, and the inference runs one way: a weaker model's failure says nothing about a frontier model. A rule that someone else froze is also a longer calendar. A model's cutoff binds what the model proposes, while a published rule is bound by the date we freeze our copy of it, and the evidence that it is worth freezing is a live record, not a backtest. This is study~2.
  \item \textbf{A faster source of independent bets.} By Grinold's law \cite{grinold1989}, $S=\mathrm{IC}\sqrt{n}$ for $n$ independent bets a year, and hundreds of co-moving perpetuals are about two directional bets, perhaps twenty once the common factor is hedged. For a forward account in bounded bets, where the 5\% cap on a stake does not bind, 80\% power within two years needs the number of independent bets a week times the squared net edge to be at least $8.59/104=0.083$: 92 bets a week at a net edge of 0.03, 33 at 0.05 and 13 at 0.08 (Appendix~\ref{app:thesis-events}). No such count has been measured for a reading agent.
  \item \textbf{Synthetic markets,} where observations are free, and which answer only whether agents search well. This is study~1.
\end{enumerate}
Overlapping bets on common factors are dependent. Dependence costs power and never validity when the judged statistic is the cash of one self-financing account (Appendix~\ref{app:thesis-events}); a weekly marked portfolio judged by a Student statistic is not valid under the same null.

\section{Two positive controls}\label{sec:program}

The wall reorders what is worth doing. A long history cannot lengthen the judged span of a model-proposed strategy, and a confirmer that sees only Sharpe 5 in a frontier model's window cannot be tested on real proposals. What can be tested are the two halves of the machinery, each against a case in which the answer should be yes. Study~1 is a positive control of the search, on synthetic markets where an edge is planted and observations are free. Study~2 is a positive control of the confirmer, on a rule that plausibly has an edge and whose judged span starts when we freeze it. In both, inference uses only data that played no part in selection, and the multiplicity paid is the number of frozen claims: one in study~1, and two rungs sharing one 5\% in study~2.

\subsection{Study 1: does the agent find an edge that exists?}\label{sec:study1}

\paragraph{The claim.} On synthetic markets in which six of twelve coins carry a planted drift set by each coin's lagged funding, the frozen agent's session ends on a program that earns from the plant with a higher chance than G100's 100 evaluations do.

\paragraph{Markets and arms.} Each market is a draw from the pilot's GARCH generator at a calibrated strength of the plant, its seed fixed in a manifest committed before any session. The plant adds the strength times a signal to each planted coin's return every bar, the signal a clipped function of that coin's funding four hours earlier. Both arms run on every study market. The agent has one session per market, never rerun, on the pilot's frozen harness and engine; it may use 100 evaluations and uses as many as it chooses. G100 uses all 100, seeded from the manifest and the market's identifier. Each arm's pick is its evaluated program with the highest score on the search period, read from the ledger the same way for both arms.

\paragraph{The measure.} A pick's quality cannot be read from its own market, on which it was selected. For each study market the measure therefore draws 57 fresh markets, each generated twice: with the plant, and at strength zero. The generator seeds every draw from the market's identifier and the strength changes no draw, so twins share funding, premiums, innovations and volume, and differ only in the planted coins' returns and, through the GARCH recursion, in those coins' variance paths. A program's effect on a pair is its score on the planted market minus its score on the twin, which is what the plant does to it. A pick \emph{earns from the plant} when, on at least 39 of its market's 57 pairs, its effect is at least half the effect of a locked reference program on the same pair and the reference's effect there is positive. If $q$ is a pick's chance of that on a fresh pair, the rule classes a pick at $q=1/2$ as earning with probability 0.0038 and one at $q=3/4$ with probability 0.901, whatever its variance or tails (Appendix~\ref{app:study1}). The reference holds all twelve coins, as every program in the language must (Appendix~\ref{app:s1choices}). A pick may earn by reading funding, by averaging past returns, which estimates a persistent drift, or by holding the drift's side. All three are discoveries of the plant, and the inputs a pick reads are reported, not judged.

\paragraph{The strength.} It is calibrated without any model. A bisection on 1{,}600 markets runs until G100's rate of earning is within 0.005 of 0.15, and a check on 10{,}000 fresh markets requires the rate there to lie between 0.12 and 0.18, which puts the true rate between 0.11 and 0.19. The target 0.15 is the highest at which the top of that band still leaves the study powered.

\paragraph{The test.} Wald's sequential sign test \cite{wald1945} runs on discordant markets, those on which exactly one arm earns, in the manifest's order: $\theta\le1/2$ against $\theta=0.7$, both errors 5\%, at most 100 markets. A discordant win adds $\log(0.7/0.5)$ and a loss $\log(0.3/0.5)$ against boundaries of $\pm\log19$, so nine straight wins claim and six straight losses stop. Under the null a discordant market is the agent's win with probability at most one half, whatever the joint law of the two arms, and by exact enumeration the size is at most 0.043. At an agent's rate of 0.5 the claim's chance is at least 0.943 across the band and every overlap of the arms' successes; at 0.4 it is 0.73 to 1.00; at 0.3 the study is not powered, and says so (Appendix~\ref{app:study1}).

\paragraph{The agent as served.} The harness asks for Claude Opus 5.5 and is answered by Opus 5.5 or, after a safeguard flag, by Opus 4.8 (Section~\ref{sec:pilot-agents}). Pinning one model would fork the harness, and rerunning flagged sessions would select on what the agent wrote, so the subject is the pilot's agent as the provider serves it: the mixture every pilot result came from. A session is the agent's when every response names one of the two models, and the report gives each session's models and the agent's rate by set of models. A session is judged on the pick it left, however it ended; one that leaves no pick, or that another model answered, is the agent's miss. Replacing an outcome by a miss never makes a claim, so failures cost power and never size.

\paragraph{Checks before any session.} Five model-free checks must pass first, and a failure sends the design back. Twins are identical except in the planted coins. The reference's effect is positive on at least 95\% of the check markets' pairs. Five locked mirror pairs of programs, each the other with every position negated, show that the measure pays only the drift captured and not what the plant does to a program and its mirror alike, such as fees against changed volatility, liquidation or ruin. The calibration's strength exists. And constant positions, which read nothing, earn on at most 14 of 200 markets, so that an agent cannot claim by submitting one without search (Appendix~\ref{app:study1}).

\paragraph{What a result may be called.} The report names the result in fixed words. At the upper boundary: \emph{the agent's sessions end on a program that earns from the plant more often than G100's 100 evaluations do}, at the shares of each model the study met. The futility stop is named ``not shown'', never ``no better''; at 100 markets without a boundary, or if the study ends early, it is inconclusive. It claims no real edge; nothing about agents in general, or about either model alone or against the other; nothing about the agent at another share of fallback; neither that a pick reads funding nor anything about the gate; and nothing about search apart from prior knowledge, since the agent's brief names the family to which the plant belongs.

\paragraph{Status.} The criterion was registered before the first session, and the study is running.

\subsection{Study 2: can the confirmer confirm a rule that plausibly has an edge?}\label{sec:study2}

\paragraph{Why a replication.} Study~1 asks whether the agent finds an edge that exists. A replication asks the matching question of the other half of the machinery: given a rule that plausibly has an edge, on evidence better than a backtest, can an exact confirmer confirm it within the calendar? It cannot show that an agent has an edge, or that the rule's record is skill: we know of the rule because it survived, so nothing recorded before our freeze judges anything. Replication of published predictors is an old design with a known finding: returns fall by about a quarter out of sample and by more than half after publication \cite{mclean2016,hou2020}.

\paragraph{The rule.} The rule is that of a public Hyperliquid vault, FuturAI Labs' ``High Sharpe, Medium Volatility'' (address \texttt{0x53375ca9\ldots2a06}), whose strategy a published analysis reverse-engineered: a long-only ladder that buys dips in large-cap coins, with a reported Sharpe ratio of 2.4 and about 1.4 recently. Its record, read only before the forward window, holds 5{,}806 fills from 3 April to 31 October 2026 in 17 coins, every one opening or closing a long and 85\% of them as maker; the published analysis reports about 11{,}000 fills, and the difference is unexplained. A cycle begins with a first buy, rests five further buys below the first fill (2.25\% to 9.85\% below, sized from 1 to 14.68 first buys), and sells the whole position at a take-profit 0.56\% above its average entry. The ladder's constants are read from fills before 1 September 2026. The trigger, which decides when a flat coin's first buy is placed, is the one part found through data, so it is fitted on fills before 1 September, checked on the two months held back, and claims no fidelity before the freeze.

\paragraph{Engine and venue model.} The replica runs in the second kernel (Section~\ref{sec:verified}) as one account at a leverage of one, with constant sizes: a first buy of \$100, and starting cash of \$50{,}000, which covers every coin's full ladder at once, so no order is refused for margin. The venue model is fixed from the recorder's design and from no record: pessimistic fills, order and observation delays of one second, a coin stale after 60 seconds without a book snapshot, an order cap of \$10{,}000 and a minimum of \$10. The optimistic rule, and a second pair of five-second delays, run beside it and judge nothing. Before 1 November 2026 the record is the venue's archive of five-second order books, with trades derived from the node's record of every fill; after the freeze it is a recorder of every perpetual's trades and five-level books at about 0.55-second intervals, which has run since 14 November 2026.

\paragraph{Fidelity.} Fidelity is defined on the vault's fills before the forward window, in three layers. First, the venue's printed arithmetic: fed the fills as given, the exact ledger must reproduce every starting position, the venue's printed fees and realized profits must be its own rounding of the ledger's exact values, and its daily funding rows must agree with the ledger's hours held, mean size and mean rate. Second, the fill rules: a real maker fill proves that an order stood at its price and a trade took it, so for each of the vault's 4{,}956 maker fills inside the trade record, the record must hold that trade, at the same price and size with the vault on the passive side; a fill inside a stale span is counted, not failed. A measurement with no pass mark places a witness order a registered delay before each real maker order's first fill and classes how late the pessimistic rule fills it, since a later fill is what the judged rule costs. Third, the trigger, which the forward run's entries must match (below). The first two are test suites, run before the freeze; the third is a registered criterion.

\paragraph{The test ledger.} The forward test judges not the account but a test ledger that copies the replica's fills. Its cash starts at 1. At each of the account's buys it buys the same lots at the same price and fee rate times a scale; at a sale it sells the fraction of its units that the account sold of its position; and it pays funding on its own units. A cycle's scale is $\varphi$ of the ledger's cash at the cycle's first buy over a registered depth $d$ in dollars; the ledger follows the cycle's first $d$ dollars of buys and nothing after, and every buy is cut to the cash there is, so the ledger never borrows and is never short. Because it trades only when the account does, at the account's prices, and no fill beats the tape (Section~\ref{sec:verified}), every scale fixed before the buy it scales is valid: the stake decides power and nothing else.

\paragraph{Two rungs on one wealth.} Two claims are made at the end of each judged day. \emph{Earned}: the ledger, started at 1 at the freeze, is worth 20 or more. Its null is that at every price event, given everything before it, the event's coin is expected to return at most nothing to a holder. \emph{Outgrew}: the ledger is worth 20 times $\bar B$, a certified bound on the wealth of the best constant mix of its coins and cash in hindsight, rebalanced at every price event at no cost, and of every coin held alone with its funding paid in cash. Its null is that some long-only, unlevered constant mix of the universe is expected to be outgrown, over each price event, by no holding chosen before that event. At a price event the ledger's wealth multiplies by $1+wr$, with $w\in[0,1]$ its share in the event's coin and $r$ the coin's return, and the event's fills can only lower it. Under the first null that factor has mean at most one, and under the second so does its ratio to the null mix's factor. The wealth and that ratio are therefore non-negative supermartingales, and each reaches 20 with probability at most 5\% under any dependence and any stopping \cite{ville1939,shafervovk2019}. Since $\bar B\ge1$, rejecting the second null implies rejecting the first, and one 5\% covers the pair. The benchmark draws on universal portfolios \cite{cover1991}, on the growth-optimal portfolio, against which every other wealth's ratio is a supermartingale \cite{long1990,covertom2006}, and on tests whose denominator is the null's best fit \cite{wasserman2020}. The bar is certified rather than optimized: an inequality on $\log(1+x)$ bounds every mix's log wealth by three sums a coin, and a convex dual gives a bound that holds at any multiplier, computed exactly at its least (Appendix~\ref{app:study2}).

\paragraph{The wealth at the bid.} The wealth the proof speaks of values coins at their prices, the mid of the latest book or a later trade, and a mid is not money: an ask pulled far up before midnight, with the bid unchanged and nothing traded, can show a large wealth for a ledger whose coins the best bid would buy for little. The rungs therefore compare the wealth at the bid, each coin valued at the lesser of its latest best bid and its price. That wealth is never above the wealth at prices, so it reaches either rung no more often, and the 5\% stands unchanged. At a gap of ten basis points from price to bid the rung moves by 0.001 in log wealth, against $\log20=3.0$.

\paragraph{What ``outgrew'' covers.} Under its null no rule passes ``outgrew'' with chance above 5\%: not a rising market, a high-beta coin, carry, the choice of coins, or rebalancing at any clock; and a holding never traded cannot pass it on any path, since the bar holds every coin held alone. But where one price event tells of the next, its coin, its sign or its time, the null is false, and a rule with no skill can outgrow every constant mix. The claim's words therefore carry that limit, and a negative control, which we call the guard, measures it from below. The guard runs two constant mixes (equal weights, and the bar's own mix, each keeping 1/25 of its wealth in cash) rebalanced at each of four clocks (a minute, five minutes, an hour and a day) on the same record. If on the 212-day replay before the freeze any of these eight rules stands 20 times over the bar at a day's end, or is on course to do so within two years (2.39 times over 212 days), ``outgrew'' is not registered. The guard can refuse the rung and never clear it: a quiet guard does not show that a record is safe.

\paragraph{Power, and the long-only wall.} The ledger is never more than fully invested, whatever the account borrows, so leverage in the account buys the test nothing. For Gaussian increments, power within two years runs from 0.00 at a yearly volatility of the ledger's wealth of 0.25 to 0.90 at 2.0 and Sharpe 2.4 (Table~\ref{tab:longonly}). The rows above full investment are out of reach, so for a long-only rule the wall is full investment: an exact test cannot see a Sharpe of 2 in two years unless the rule's fully staked profit is as volatile as a coin, all the time. Where the ledger's wealth sits in that table depends on how much of the ladder it copies (Appendix~\ref{app:longonly}). The table is an upper reference, since a dip buyer's returns are skewed against it. The study's operating power is forecast before the freeze by a block bootstrap of the replay, which also chooses the stake among 30 pairs of $\varphi$ and $d$ by a rule fixed in advance (Appendix~\ref{app:study2}). The forward test is therefore expected to end ``not confirmed''.

\paragraph{The forward run as a measurement.} We run it anyway, for what nothing else measures: whether the volatility the wall was computed from holds on dates nobody fitted, how the fill rules behave against the vault's forward fills on recorded books and trades, and whether the trigger matches. Two more criteria are registered. \emph{The volatility held} if the variance of the ledger's daily returns at prices over the first 212 judged days is within a factor of two of its variance over the 212-day replay. That is a factor of $\sqrt2$ in volatility, half the way between two rows of the power table; it is a fixed tolerance, because a block bootstrap's 95\% interval for this ratio covers it as rarely as 63\% of the time where variance persists. \emph{The entries matched} if, over the first 61 judged days, more than half of the replica's entries and more than half of the vault's are matched one to one on the same coin within six minutes, the time by which a tenth of the vault's cycles have ended. Only that criterion licenses calling the forward rule one most of whose entries matched the vault's.

\paragraph{Timing.} The freeze is a registration, published the day it is made, and the judged days are the 730 days from the first midnight after it. The forward record is read only after the protected window ends on 31 January 2027, and it is replayed once a month from February 2027. The test is final at ``outgrew'' or after the 730th judged day, and a record that stops ends it at the last day run.

\paragraph{What is said, and what is not.} Every claim says ``assuming our orders move nobody'': a taker fill does not thin the next book, and every recorded trade would have happened as recorded. Every claim also says that the 5\% holds if no settlement charged a unit more than its price, that funding is paid at each coin's price where the venue uses its oracle's, and that fees are charged at the schedule's rate with nothing cut. No claim is of skill, of the replica's profit being the vault's, of the bid taking the ledger's size, or of any other rule, stake or venue. A non-rejection is ``not confirmed'', reported with the wealth at the bid and at prices, the bar, the guard's figures, the realized volatility and Sharpe, and the power row; it is never ``no edge''.

\paragraph{Status.} The three criteria are registered. The engine, the replica and the runner are being built and adversarially reviewed, and the study has not frozen. Until it does, nothing dated from 1 November 2026 on is read by anyone who decides whether or when to freeze.

\subsection{What stays closed, and what would reopen it}\label{sec:closed}

\paragraph{Reading agents.} The direction we first judged most promising for a reading agent was event-driven macro: theses about how a scheduled or unscheduled event will move a specific exposure over days to weeks, where reading and synthesis matter most and speed least. A language model's judgment cannot be backtested on a period its training may cover, so such theses are registered before their outcomes and judged only forward. We built that track on Hyperliquid's perpetuals, with a retrieval log of every document read, sizing to a fixed risk budget, and scores against historical and no-reading comparators. Independent checks of its scoring agreed after two revisions, and seven frozen versions ran without any completing its window. In the six sessions counted, three of the reading agent and three of a comparison agent without documents, neither registered a thesis. A forward study of it needs a measured 33 near-independent bets a week at a net edge of 0.05 (Section~\ref{sec:moves}), on a venue whose terms permit the study. The event-contract venues we examined restrict use of their data to a member's own trading, require written approval, or forbid automated collection. The track is parked until both conditions hold. Pairing a reading agent with the same agent without its documents is fair but buys no power: where the arms agree the difference carries nothing, so the law of Section~\ref{sec:moves} holds in discordant forecasts, 859 of them if reading adds five points of hit rate and 3{,}437 if it adds two and a half.

\paragraph{Earlier cutoffs.} A campaign with a model whose cutoff is two years old moves the confirmable Sharpe to about 2 and reads only one way. It waits until study~1 reaches its upper boundary and a confirmer with the rule's own loss bound exists.

\paragraph{Long histories.} We had planned research on long histories of equities, indices, commodities and rates, with builder-deployed venue markets as an execution mapping certified separately. For a strategy a language model proposes, a long history lengthens the search period and not the judged one, so that work was superseded before any of it was judged. Three of its principles stand. A universe is defined point in time, since today's listed markets were chosen by past popularity. A result on an underlying benchmark is not a backtest of a venue market, whose returns add funding, basis, mark-price dynamics and liquidation. And a source's results move from a signal study to an executable backtest only on newly registered evidence. Every direction we considered, from event-driven theses and broad premia such as time-series momentum \cite{moskowitz2012,hurst2017} to small-capacity effects, venue structure and slowly absorbed information, is under the wall a frozen rule, confirmable forward only near Sharpe 2 within two years.

\paragraph{Machinery not in use.} The pilot's gate, its swarm and the extension of its language to declared sources are parked until a study needs them, and none is used again with a known defect open against it (Appendix~\ref{app:defects}). The pilot's forward window lapses on 31 January 2027 with nothing registered in it, and its prices stay unread.

\subsection{Stop rules}\label{sec:stop}

Study~1 ends at a boundary or at 100 markets, with no extension; it would not have started had a model-free check failed. Study~2 does not freeze if a check before the freeze fails, and its forward test ends at its second rung or two years after the freeze. When study~1 has reported and study~2's fidelity is in, the program stops for review before anything else begins. Nothing is rescued by lowering a threshold, pooling instances, reading a protected window early, adding a rung, or calling a non-rejection an absence.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

Agentic research automation is not new. The possible contribution here is narrower: a restricted strategy language with proved core invariants, typed and certified sources, a full record of what agents saw, point-in-time evaluation, multiplicity-aware inference calibrated by null simulation, and exact confirmers, combined in one system, together with a quantitative account of what such a system can confirm. Establishing that the combination adds something requires a direct comparison with the systems below, which this paper does not make.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Automated quant research.} R\&D-Agent-Quant \cite{li2025} runs a closed loop of hypothesis generation, code development, market evaluation and feedback. Alpha-GPT \cite{wang2023} mines alpha factors interactively with language models. Kakushadze's 101 Formulaic Alphas \cite{kakushadze2016} publishes formulaic alphas used in industry.
  \item \textbf{Backtesting infrastructure.} Event-driven engines such as QuantConnect's LEAN \cite{lean} use a time frontier to limit access to future data and separate fee and fill models. A restricted language must offer stronger, stated guarantees to justify itself.
  \item \textbf{Multiple testing in finance.} Harvey, Liu and Zhu \cite{harvey2016} argue for a $t$-statistic hurdle near 3 for new factors. Bailey and L\'opez de Prado introduce the deflated Sharpe ratio \cite{bailey2014} and the minimum track record length \cite{bailey2012}, and with Borwein and Zhu the probability of backtest overfitting \cite{bailey2017}. White \cite{white2000}, Hansen \cite{hansen2005} and Romano and Wolf \cite{romano2005} give bootstrap tests for data snooping, and Sullivan, Timmermann and White \cite{sullivan1999} apply them to technical trading rules; the effects with long records are broad premia such as time-series momentum \cite{moskowitz2012,hurst2017}. Replications of published anomalies find that returns fall out of sample and after publication \cite{mclean2016,hou2020}.
  \item \textbf{Adaptive data analysis, data splitting and sequential testing.} Dwork et al.\ \cite{dwork2015} reuse a holdout under adaptive queries with formal guarantees, for independent samples rather than dependent time series. Cox \cite{cox1975} analyses the split-sample design the gate uses. Foster and Stine \cite{foster2008} give alpha-investing for sequences of tests, and Wald's sequential probability ratio test \cite{wald1945} underlies study~1's stopping rule.
  \item \textbf{Testing by betting.} Ville's inequality \cite{ville1939} and Kelly's criterion \cite{kelly1956} underlie the exact tests here. Shafer \cite{shafer2021}, Shafer and Vovk \cite{shafervovk2019}, and Waudby-Smith and Ramdas \cite{waudbysmith2024} develop testing and estimation by betting. Universal portfolios \cite{cover1991}, the numeraire portfolio \cite{long1990,covertom2006} and universal inference \cite{wasserman2020} inform study~2's benchmark.
  \item \textbf{Forecasting with language models.} Recent work evaluates language-model forecasters on questions resolved after their training cutoffs \cite{halawi2024,karger2025}, and Prophet Arena \cite{prophetarena2025} compares them with market prices.
  \item \textbf{Formal methods.} Equality saturation \cite{willsey2021} supports detecting equivalent expressions, Lean~4 \cite{demoura2021} provides the proof checker, and CompCert \cite{leroy2009} shows what a refinement proof connecting a formal semantics to an implementation involves.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

The weakest link is non-stationarity: every inference method here relies in some way on the sample being informative about the future, and markets drift and adapt. Beyond it:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Multiplicity under adaptivity.} Adaptive-inference theory exists, but no general method turns an agent swarm's interaction history into a trustworthy multiplicity estimate. Null simulation of the whole procedure is the practical check, and inference is confined to untouched data.
  \item \textbf{Overfitting to the null simulator.} A gate refined until it passes its own null models may fail on others. Null models used while designing the gate are kept separate from locked suites, which are specified independently and run only to judge.
  \item \textbf{Error across campaigns.} Per-campaign control does not bound the project's lifetime count of false promotions (Section~\ref{sec:gate}).
  \item \textbf{Incomplete canonicalization.} Behavioral equivalence is undecidable for expressive languages, though decidable for finite-state Mealy machines over finite alphabets. A language with real-valued state is on the hard side, and clustering by return correlation is only a similarity heuristic.
  \item \textbf{Expressiveness.} A language strict enough to forbid leakage may forbid useful strategies. An extension is acceptable only if it preserves the stated invariants and the proved theorems.
  \item \textbf{Trust in the proof checker.} A soundness bug in the proof checker would vouch for leaky strategies. Lean's kernel is small and mature, its proofs can be rechecked by independently implemented kernels, and each proof's axioms are listed.
  \item \textbf{Hindsight in extracted data.} Masked validation reduces, but does not remove, the chance that an extractor's outputs reflect what its model knows about later events.
  \item \textbf{Data and terms.} Availability times of text sources are hard to establish after the fact, archives can be revised or incomplete, and many sources' terms are silent on automated use, which is not permission.
  \item \textbf{A hosted agent.} A provider can change the model that answers, or the safeguard that redirects a session, on any day. A change it does not disclose is invisible to any study of a hosted model.
  \item \textbf{Paper fills.} A replay on recorded books assumes the book was there to hit and that our orders move nobody. Study~2's comparison with an account that really traded is the first check of that, and a forward result remains a claim about the frozen replica.
  \item \textbf{Recordings that stop.} A forward test judges recordings that cannot be fetched again. A gap is staleness, in which nothing fills, and a recording that stops ends the test early.
  \item \textbf{A design not yet frozen.} Study~2's specification is still under adversarial review. From its freeze, a defect found in the engine or in the code between the record and a verdict ends the study.
  \item \textbf{Venue risk.} Smart-contract, oracle, deployer and liquidity risks can be partly modeled, but their severe tails are poorly represented in short samples.
  \item \textbf{An unmeasured prior.} The net effect a skilled forecaster keeps on a priced outcome is taken as at most 0.05 a bet. That is a prior. The published benchmarks \cite{halawi2024,karger2025,prophetarena2025} find language-model forecasters at or below crowds and markets, and they test forecasting on given questions, not selection among markets.
  \item \textbf{Slow forward evidence.} Forward weeks under a frozen protocol cannot be bought later, and a hosted model may be retired before a two-year test of it ends.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A backtest is not a proof checker, and no engineering makes it one. We built what engineering can supply: a language that rules out specified leaks, a kernel that records everything an agent saw, certificates for every source, a gate whose whole procedure is calibrated on markets with and without edge, and proofs of the language's invariants, now carried down to the code that runs. The pilot showed this machinery working in the direction in which it can be tested: on markets without an edge it promoted nothing. It also met the limit that engineering cannot remove. A strategy proposed by a frontier model can be judged only after the model's cutoff, and over months an exact test needs a Sharpe ratio near six to confirm anything. The two positive controls ask what can still be learned inside that limit: whether the agent finds an edge that exists, where observations are free, and whether an exact confirmer confirms a rule with a live record, where the calendar starts at our freeze. Their results, whichever way they fall, will be reported in words fixed before the evidence.

The appendices hold the paper's derivations and the reasons for its choices. \emph{Derived} means a calculation under named premises, not a universal validation. \emph{Choice} means a policy or model convention, given with its reason. \emph{Owed} marks a stronger claim that has not been derived. \subsection{The noise ceiling (derived illustration)}\label{app:noise}

For independent, stationary Gaussian returns near zero Sharpe, the annualized standard error of a Sharpe estimate over $T$ years is about $T^{-1/2}$. Choosing $T=5$, 1{,}000 null trials, a threshold of 1 and ten real trials of true Sharpe 0.5 illustrates selection: $1-\Phi(\sqrt5)=0.0127$ gives 12.7 expected null acceptances, against $10[1-\Phi(0.5\sqrt5)]\simeq1.3$ real ones. About 90\% of the expected acceptances are null; that is a ratio of expected counts, not an exact expected false discovery proportion. For independent $Z_i\sim N(0,1)$,
\[
 \E\max_i\Shat_i=\SE\,\E\max_i Z_i,\qquad
 \E\max_i Z_i\sim\sqrt{2\log N},\qquad h(N,T)=\E\max_i Z_i/\sqrt T.
\]
Table~\ref{tab:ceiling} integrates the maximum's density $N\phi(z)\Phi(z)^{N-1}$. The leading term $\sqrt{2\log N}$ overstates it by 39\%, 15\% and 9\% at $N=10$, 1{,}000 and 100{,}000: at $N=1{,}000$ and $T=5$ it gives 1.66 against $3.24/\sqrt5=1.45$. The table's grid of trials and years is illustrative, not a calibrated screen, and dependence and non-stationarity make a literal reading of it invalid.

\subsection{The deflated Sharpe ratio (derived, qualified)}\label{app:dsr}

For $T$ per-period returns, let $\Shat$ be their mean over their sample standard deviation (divisor $T-1$), $m_k=T^{-1}\sum(r-\bar r)^k$, $\hat\gamma_3=m_3/m_2^{3/2}$, $\hat\gamma_4=m_4/m_2^2$ (ordinary kurtosis), and $\gamma_E$ the Euler--Mascheroni constant. Then
\[
 \mathrm{DSR}=\Phi\left(\frac{(\Shat-S_0)\sqrt{T-1}}
 {\sqrt{1-\hat\gamma_3\Shat+(\hat\gamma_4-1)\Shat^2/4}}\right),\quad
 S_0=\frac{(1-\gamma_E)\Phi^{-1}(1-1/N)+\gamma_E\Phi^{-1}(1-1/(Ne))}{\sqrt{T-1}},
\]
with $S_0=0$ for $N=1$. A per-period Sharpe estimate has asymptotic variance $(1-\gamma_3S+(\gamma_4-1)S^2/4)/(T-1)$, and at $S=0$, the least favorable point of the null, its reference scale is $1/\sqrt{T-1}$ for any regular martingale-difference returns, which is what a strategy's net return is on the synthetic markets. That needs finite variance, a martingale central limit theorem and no dominating observations; even the zero-Sharpe Gaussian finite-sample variance is $(T-1)/(T(T-3))$ for $T>3$. The numerator uses Bailey and L\'opez de Prado's approximation of an expected maximum, not an exact distribution of the maximum. Costs, negative means, heavy tails, adaptivity and negatively correlated trials each rule out a universal conservative reading, and autocorrelation changes the sampling behavior \cite{lo2002}. The observed dispersion of Sharpe ratios across trials is not a null scale: it was the pilot's first benchmark, and there a real edge raised its own hurdle (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}). Annualization divides an annual Sharpe by $\sqrt{252}$ for a trading-day convention, $\sqrt{365}$ for daily continuous markets, or $\sqrt{6\cdot365}$ for the pilot's four-hour steps.

Pearson's inequality $\gamma_4\ge\gamma_3^2+1$ makes the variance term non-negative, but at a two-point boundary it is numerically unstable, and two independent implementations disagreed exactly there. A variance term at most $10^{-12}(1+|\hat\gamma_3\Shat|+(\hat\gamma_4-1)\Shat^2/4)$ is treated as degenerate and gives zero, a \emph{choice} to refuse scores that roundoff could change, not a proved error budget. Computing the moments on $r/\max|r|$ preserves the Sharpe ratio, skewness and kurtosis and avoids overflow, and a non-finite return is an error, never a score. The threshold of 0.95 is a chosen screen, not a confidence statement.

\subsection{Betting, absorption and multiplicity (derived under scope)}\label{app:bet}

Let $r'_t$ be integrable pre-absorption net returns with $\E[r'_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]\le0$ while the account is alive, stopped at bankruptcy, and choose predictable $\lambda_t\in[0,1/2]$. Under the lower bound $r'_t\ge-L$ with $L=2$, every factor $1+\lambda_tr'_t\ge1-L/2=0$ is non-negative, and $K_t=\prod_{s\le t}(1+\lambda_sr'_s)$ is a non-negative supermartingale from 1. A bankruptcy's reported return of $-1$ and the pre-absorption terminal loss cannot create a new maximum, and later reported returns are zero, so the two running maxima agree (row~3 of Table~\ref{tab:defects}). Ville's inequality \cite{ville1939} gives $p=\min(1,1/\max K)$ with
\[
 P(p_i\le u\mid\mathcal F_{\rm search},H_{0,i})\le u.
\]
Holm's correction then bounds any false rejection in the frozen family at the chosen $\alpha=0.05$, without assuming independent finalists, provided the finalists are frozen on search information alone. The pilot's reported floor of $-1$ follows from absorption and zero subsequent equity. It does not imply the conditional null, because
\[
 \E[\max(r',-1)\mid\mathcal F]=\E[r'\mid\mathcal F]+\E[(-1-r')_+\mid\mathcal F]
\]
(row~1), and conditioning on never going bankrupt does not restore it. The corrected synthetic markets establish $r'\ge-2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:loss}); in real markets the bound is assumed and owed, and rare observed bankruptcies do not certify it.

The bet is $\lambda_1=0$, then $\min(1/2,\max(0,S_{t-1}/Q_{t-1}))$, zero if $Q=0$, with $S=\sum r$ and $Q=\sum r^2$. Maximizing the second-order approximation $\E\log(1+\lambda r)\simeq\lambda\mu-\lambda^2\E r^2/2$ gives $\mu/\E r^2$, which motivates the plug-in. The cap $1/2=1/L$ is derived, not chosen: Ville's non-negativity requires $\lambda_t\le1/L$ under $r'\ge-L$. Only the plug-in form inside $[0,1/2]$ is a choice (row~6). As written in binary64, the rule overflows or underflows on extreme sums that no campaign produces.

For independent campaigns each at 5\%, twenty give a $1-0.95^{20}\simeq64\%$ chance of some false promotion. A project-wide guarantee follows instead from a union bound when $\sum_j\alpha_j\le\alpha$, for example with $\alpha_j=\alpha2^{-j}$. The pilot's two-arm agent run corrected within each arm, so its union bound is twice the per-arm level, not a joint 5\% claim. A finalist missing from the frozen batch stays at $p=1$, because removing it could lower Holm's hurdles.

\subsection{Calibration sample sizes (derived bounds, chosen targets)}\label{app:calibration}

For $x$ promotions in $n$ independent complete-null campaigns, the one-sided Clopper--Pearson upper endpoint $q$ solves $P_q(X\le x)=0.05$. With $x=0$, $q=1-0.05^{1/n}$: bringing it below 5\% needs 59 runs, and below 1\% needs 299. The calibration chose 300 runs per cell, with targets of 5\% for promotion and 10\% for reaching the finalist path. Since $P_q(X\le x)$ falls in $q$, $q(x)\le q_0$ exactly when $P_{q_0}(X\le x)\le0.05$. At $n=300$, $P_{0.05}(X\le8)=0.0341\le0.05<0.0650=P_{0.05}(X\le9)$, so at most eight promotions meet the 5\% target; likewise $P_{0.10}(X\le21)=0.0458\le0.05<0.0699=P_{0.10}(X\le22)$, so at most 21 meet the 10\% target. None in 300 bounds the rate at 0.0099. The single agent's twenty campaigns, allowing at most two counted events, bound only about 28\% (none would bound 14\%), and the swarm's ten, allowing at most one, about 39\% (none would bound 26\%). These choices keep expensive workloads affordable. They support smoke claims only, not a 5\% calibration.

For a campaign's false discovery proportion $X\in[0,1]$, Hoeffding's inequality gives $\E X\le\bar X+\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/(2n)}$ with confidence $1-\delta$. The planted sweep chose $n=100$, $\delta=0.05$ and a target of 0.20, so it requires $\bar X\le0.20-\sqrt{\log20/200}\simeq0.0776$. Any gate that promotes in at most 7 of 100 campaigns meets that cutoff: the criterion bounds the false discovery rate, not the share of promotions that are false.

The locked suites for the holdout test use 20{,}000 series per cell, lengths of 540, 546 and 1{,}080 steps, and levels of 0.05, 0.01 and 0.005. Each judged rate's upper confidence bound, at confidence $1-0.05/K$ to cover all $K$ judged combinations (54 in the first two suites, 99 in the third), must lie below a chosen tolerance of 0.0625, 0.015 or 0.01. The suite therefore certifies the tolerance and not the nominal level. Up to 1{,}138, 244 and 154 rejections in 20{,}000 pass, so a true rate of 0.056 at level 0.05 passes 72\% of the time, 0.012 at 0.01 passes 62\%, and 0.0075 at 0.005 passes 65\% (row~7). The nominal levels rest on the proof and the return bound, and the suites cannot establish conditional validity outside their nulls.

\subsection{Holdout power (derived approximation, not a universal limit)}\label{app:power}

At a saturated half cap, $\log K_n\simeq n(\mu/2-\E r^2/8)$, so, ignoring the quadratic cost, one finalist needs $\mu\simeq2\log20/n$ to reach $p=0.05$: about 1.66\%, 1.10\%, 0.55\% and 0.11\% a step at $n=360$, 546, 1{,}080 and 5{,}400. Holm's correction raises that hurdle. An unconstrained second-order Kelly approximation, $\log K\simeq nS_{\rm step}^2/2$, holds only when Kelly's fraction $\mu/\sigma^2$ is within the cap of one half; at 2\% a step that is $\mu\le0.0002$, an annualized Sharpe of at most $0.01\sqrt{2190}=0.47$. With the cap, and at 80\% power, the gate needs a Sharpe of 27.6 at 546 steps, 7.4 at one year and 4.0 at two (Table~\ref{tab:wallmatch}). Neither approximation is a theorem that every valid test needs that effect, and the nonexistence of a uniformly powerful test over unrestricted finite-mean distributions \cite{bahadur1956} is a different statement (row~6).

The sensitivity target of 30\% is a \emph{choice}, not derived from any use. The planted Sharpe ratios of 1, 2 and 3 are chosen scenarios, and the regime-switching cell's 3 was amended to 2.5 because 3 was unattainable on its calibration curve. A cell with a 0.7\% mean, chosen from a grid that considered the test alone, was suspended; the 900-day cell with a 0.2\% mean was chosen from a grid of effects the pipeline could reach with high test power. Neither met the target, and the waiver stands.

\subsection{Selection rules and workloads (choices)}\label{app:selection}

The deflated Sharpe threshold of 0.95, the 5\% levels of SPA and the stepdown, the stationary bootstrap's mean block of six four-hour steps (row~8) and its 1{,}000 resamples are \emph{choices}: a stringent screen and a reproducible, affordable heuristic for dependence, not cutoffs derived from a claim. The raw count of filled trials is $N$ so that no favorable estimate is chosen. The complete-linkage cuts at $|\rho|$ of 0.9 and 0.5 are chosen contrasts for fragility, not bounds on the effective number of trials, and a trial with zero variance is a singleton. The mechanism check, a positive difference between the sample means of the branches a prediction names, matches its stated sample claim (row~5); requiring each branch to hold 10\% to 90\% of the exposure is an underived choice that keeps the claim from being vacuous. Owed is an argument about precision and power that admits genuinely rare mechanisms.

The agent's budget of 100 evaluations, the baselines' 100 and 400, the swarm's four agents with 400 in all and at most 100 each, 20 calls for history, up to ten candidates of the agent's choosing, 200 turns and two hours define a chosen workload that the pilot could afford. They neither bound the information agents receive nor guarantee an adequate opportunity to research. The gate's $N$ counts only distinct canonical strategies that filled at least once. History is logged because it changes later selection. A strategy's stated prediction is left out of its canonical key, because identity tracks trading behavior, not an explanatory filter. Evidence from the baseline searchers does not calibrate the richer disclosures an agent receives. The swarm's tolerance of one unresolved and one unestablished campaign is a chosen smoke policy, and a promotion could mask a campaign that was not established, a gap reported but not repaired.

\section{Execution and the pilot's kernel}\label{app:engine}

\subsection{Grid, fills and costs (derived timing, chosen model)}\label{app:execution}

The pilot chose hourly feature ticks, four-hour decisions, an initial equity of \$10{,}000, a target gross of 2, an order cap of \$10{,}000 and cost buckets of \$1{,}000 and \$10{,}000, so that a small account is feasible on coarse history. The four-hour history is the finest retained execution grid. If the close at boundary $B$ arrives at $B+\delta$, the opening price at $B$ predates the decision, and the first recorded open after it is at $B+4$ hours. An empty selected bar does not roll forward, since rolling would silently choose a different execution opportunity. Settlement precedes fills at equal instants, so that the position that actually held the hour pays for it (row~4). These conventions make no claim about queue position or executable liquidity.

For reference notional $a$, adverse slippage fraction $s$ and fee $f$, a buy costs $as+af(1+s)=a(s+f+sf)$, and a sell's cost, $a(s+f-sf)$, is bounded by the same $\kappa=s+f+sf$. The base taker fee of 0.00045, the hourly funding cap of 0.04, the size decimals, the \$10 minimum order and the maintenance tiers are the venue's rules, not fitted thresholds. Applying the highest documented fee and maintenance to history whose rules are unknown is a model choice, not proved conservative for a full replay. Sizes are truncated toward zero to the venue's granularity, an exact zero target closes dust, and a year of 365 days of 24 hours is a conversion convention, not a market calendar.

\subsection{A loss bound for corrected synthetic markets (derived, limited domain)}\label{app:loss}

Choose the account's own stop-out at $\Lambda=4$ times equity, distinct from the venue's liquidation; together with fill admission it bounds held exposure, which normalizing targets alone does not. Let $e_{\rm prev}>0$ be the equity recorded before a step's fills, $G_0,G_1$ the gross exposure at the marks before and after those fills, and $m$ the largest next-bar carrying loss per unit of notional through funding and an adverse closeout, its cost included. Assume that opens equal marks without gaps, that any delisting closeout is at the mark, and that $\Lambda\kappa\le1$. After a stop-out either nothing is held or gross at adverse prices is within $\Lambda$ times equity. Moves in the position's favor preserve that bound at the marks. A closeout at the mark removes notional $a$ and costs at most $\kappa a$, preserving it since $\Lambda\kappa\le1$, and a reduction that keeps the sign does the same; other fills require margin admission, and fills only lower equity. So $G_0,G_1\le\Lambda e_{\rm prev}$. At most one fill a coin trades at most $G_0+G_1$, costing $2\Lambda\kappa e_{\rm prev}$, and carrying costs at most $mG_1$. Therefore
\[
 r'\ge-\Lambda(2\kappa+m),\qquad
 \Lambda=4,\quad m\le\tfrac12-2\kappa\ \Longrightarrow\ r'\ge-2.
\]
This is reviewed algebra, not a machine-checked theorem (row~4). Real gaps break the premise of fills at the mark, and observing no excursion says nothing about the tails. The choice of 4 leaves headroom above the target gross of 2: a single long at 2 reaches the stop-out after a one-third adverse move, since $2(1-d)/(1-2d)=4$ at $d=1/3$.

Corrected synthetic markets choose $|\varepsilon|\le0.3$, wick multipliers $|u|\le0.1$ and $\kappa\le0.003$ as sufficient design bounds, not as the only values that work. With hourly $|f|\le0.04$,
\[
 c/o\le(1+3\cdot0.04+0.3)/(1-0.04)<1.48.
\]
An adverse closeout loses at most $0.3+0.1\cdot1.48<0.448$ before its cost when the bar moves against the position, or $0.1+3\cdot0.04+0.04\cdot1.48<0.28$ when it moves for it. The closeout's cost adds at most $1.1\cdot1.48\kappa$, so $m<0.46\le1/2-2\kappa$, and lows stay at least $0.9\min(o,c)>0$. The pilot's original generators bounded $|\varepsilon|$ by 0.5, which kept closes positive, but their unbounded wicks and the frozen engine's held exposure do not establish the bound. The clips change every null and planted path, so the pilot's calibration does not carry over to corrected markets.

\subsection{Cost calibration (choices and debts)}\label{app:costs}

A constant per coin, side and size bucket is a \emph{choice} forced by sparse bars and the lack of historical full-depth features: a day holds only six four-hour bars per coin, too few to condition on a bar's state. The one-day recording, the 70/30 chronological split, the fit at the 95th percentile, the 90\% coverage, at least one snapshot a minute and the slack of 3 basis points are \emph{underived choices} for a tractable, conservative snapshot model, not bounds on costs at selected trades. For $n$ ordered test costs, $q_{\lceil0.9n\rceil}$ is \emph{derived} as the least constant covering 90\%, an unfillable order counting as infinite; replacing the median excess by the model less that quantile measures extra conservatism, not median accuracy. Higher costs lower profit on a fixed path, but equity-dependent sizing prevents extending that to full replays. For a delisted coin with no live book, the cell-wise maximum over the other coins is an unvalidated fallback, not an economic bound. Owed are coverage conditioned on trades, a tolerance on the error of returns, sampling uncertainty and transfer across markets. The doubled-cost report is a chosen stress contrast, and the archive comparison's thresholds (one second, 50\% coverage, 95\% agreement) were screens for the fidelity of a proxy, not guarantees of execution.

\subsection{Language and numerical policies}\label{app:kernel}

Strategies as JSON syntax trees, no access to host code, clocks or paths, strict fields, references only to earlier bindings, explicit conversion of time units, undefined rather than invented missing values, and evaluation of every node at every tick are \emph{choices} that make causality, state and the information boundary checkable. A non-finite node output becomes undefined, so no target is non-finite, and an undefined weight holds the position rather than inventing a signal, while the positions so held reserve gross budget. The corrected specification also sizes on equity after fills, so that costs cannot leave the next target on a budget that has vanished; keeps a bankrupt account and its pending orders from reviving within a reporting window; and assigns a boundary fill to the window whose returns bear its costs and a closing fill to the exposure it closes, a reversal belonging to the new exposure, so that the mechanism compares fully net returns. Section~\ref{sec:pilot-checks} lists where the frozen engine departs from it. Population window variance, an exponential average with $\alpha=2/(n+1)$ and seeding from the first defined value are conventional smoothing choices that guarantee nothing about stationarity. Conversion factors carry their units, so equal numeric factors do not make two units interchangeable. The proofs quantify over numeric structures because binary64 algebra is not field algebra, and the executable comparisons cover sampled binary64 behavior; they are not a refinement proof.

Limits of 1\,MiB of text, 4{,}096 nodes, depth 64 and lags of 4{,}380 hours are choices of resources and scope, not causality requirements. Window sums are recomputed every window length, trading cost against accumulated roundoff, and a slack of $10^{-12}$ in the portfolio's scaling is a chosen floating-point guard without a full roundoff budget. The lookahead canary's Gaussian log returns have mean $-\sigma^2/2$, derived from $\E e^X=e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}$ so that prices are a martingale. Its 2\% volatility, 550 days, admitted and leaking Sharpe ratios of 4 and 20, and its seeds and random programs have no joint calibration of false alarms and detection. Over 550 days the standard error of an annualized Sharpe is 0.81, so a leak worth Sharpe 2 stays under the alarm at 4 in 99.3\% of single runs: the canary detects gross lookahead only.

\subsection{Exact rewrites}\label{app:rewrites}

The canonicalizer admits only identities justified locally on finite stored binary64 values. Double negation restores sign bits, repeated absolute value clears them once, and absolute value after negation clears the flipped bit. Multiplying or dividing by a dimensionless 1 and subtracting a same-unit $+0$ are exact, $-0$ included. Addition and multiplication commute bit for bit, and their unit rules are symmetric. Strict \emph{and} and \emph{or} commute because both operands are read, and a typed boolean is 0 or 1, so double \emph{not} is exact. Swapping branches while negating the condition selects the same value, an undefined condition staying undefined, and both branches' state still ticks. Comparison dualities compare the same finite operands. Propagation of undefined values, and unchanged inputs to the stateful descendants that remain, lift these local identities to trading behavior. The rewrite-soundness theorem proves the abstract version under these obligations; it does not prove IEEE arithmetic itself.

Counterexamples explain what is excluded. Associating $(10^{16}+(-10^{16}))+1$ gives 1 against 0; distributing $0.3(0.1+0.2)$ changes rounding by one unit in the last place; $-0+(+0)=+0$, so the additive identity is unsafe; and minimum and maximum ties on opposite signed zeros depend on operand order. An undefined $c$ makes $\mathrm{if}(c,x,x)$ undefined, and a strict $\mathrm{and}(\mathrm{false},c)$ undefined. Demeaning $[1,2,4]$ leaves a mean of about $-1.5\cdot10^{-16}$, so a second demeaning changes bits. Converting $0.9452706955539223$ by 8{,}760 and back yields $0.9452706955539222$. Moving a lag across windows or cross-sections changes recompute epochs or membership, and $\mathrm{neg}(\mathrm{sign}(0))=-0$ differs from $\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{neg}(0))=+0$. No law fusing lags is admitted.

\subsection{Canonical keys, tolerances and test inventories}\label{app:kernel-checks}

Limits on nodes and iterations rather than on wall time, then extraction of the fewest nodes with a lexical tie-break, are \emph{choices} for reproducible keys; they do not give complete semantic equivalence. The 30 iterations and 20{,}000 nodes, and the requirement that 99\% of 1{,}000 generated and 20 hand-built equivalent pairs merge, have no derivation of coverage risk. Identity pins versions and float bits, because limits and rounding can change keys. Appending to the ledger precedes disclosure, so a failed commit cannot leave unlogged research. Result hashes detect stale corruption, not an adversary who can rewrite both the schema and the hashes, so untrusted code holds no writable connection to the ledger.

The engine's reconciliation tolerance is $10^{-9}\max(10^4,|e|,\max_{s<t}|e_s|)$ dollars: reconciliation terms keep the roundoff of the magnitudes they once reached, even after equity collapses, so scaling by current equity rejected correct runs. The coefficient is chosen, not derived from a bound on the count of operations, and it can hide large later errors. The comparison of the reference with the engine is bit-exact; the kernel checks' other budgets, of $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-9}$ dollars, are chosen and establish no mathematical equivalence. The frozen corpus's 550 days give 13{,}200 hourly ticks, enough for three nested lags of 4{,}380 hours but not four, so a fresh corpus owes coverage of each program's warm-up. The test inventories (a mutant per invariant, tens of adversarial programs, eight named hand scenarios, a thousand generated programs or rewrites) are sized to be tractable while covering varied adversarial and boundary cases; they do not bound the prevalence of defects not yet seen. The bound of 100\,ms on the witness's median time checks the pilot's throughput, not its correctness.

\section{Synthetic markets and the pilot's data}\label{app:dataapp}

\subsection{Synthetic markets with no edge (derived; model choices explicit)}\label{app:nulls}

Write $f_1,\dots,f_4$ for a bar's hourly funding rates, the first three settled at its open and the fourth at the next bar's open. Under that settlement order (row~4), a predictable position $q$ held through a bar earns
\[
 q(c-o)-qo(f_1+f_2+f_3)-qcf_4.
\]
Setting $c=o(1+f_1+f_2+f_3+\varepsilon)/(1-f_4)$, substitution gives $qo\varepsilon$. Fresh conditionally symmetric noise, even symmetrically clipped, has $\E[\varepsilon\mid\mathcal F_o]=0$ given the entire observable past of every coin. Quantity, cap, rounding and fill admission are measurable at the open, so conditioning gives zero expected gross profit before absorption. A long liquidation adds $q(\mathrm{low}-c)\le0$ and a short one $q(\mathrm{high}-c)\le0$, and fees and slippage only subtract, so the pre-absorption net conditional profit is non-positive; the reported, absorbed profit need not be (Appendix~\ref{app:bet}). Event inputs bootstrapped from an independent random stream reveal no future innovation, so the argument holds; replaying price-linked events without that independence would break it.

The Gaussian generator's 2\% scale truncated at eight standard deviations; the GARCH generator's common factor and innovations, each a mix of two truncated $t_5$ draws at loading $\lambda$ (the mix is not $t_5$, and before truncation its kurtosis is $9-12\lambda^2(1-\lambda^2)$); and the regime-switching generator's two states of volatility and correlation with symmetric jumps are \emph{model choices} that span simple, persistent, heavy-tailed and regime-dependent volatility, all without a predictable mean. Twelve coins, 100 trades a bar, lognormal volume with log-mean 10 and log-standard deviation 0.5, Gaussian wicks at half the bar's scale, a volatility cap of $0.0625^2$, a jump cap of 0.2 and five-sigma jump selection are chosen finite envelopes, not empirically sufficient coverage of markets. Scaling truncated innovations by their actual truncated standard deviation gives the stated unit variance. The regime-switching generator clips its jumps where its specification truncates them: symmetry survives, but the jumps' distribution and moments differ. Variance targeting uses $\omega=s^2(1-\alpha-\beta)$, derived from the stationary GARCH variance, which keeps near-integrated fits from overstating the target. For unit-variance independent factors $x_i=\lambda z_m+\sqrt{1-\lambda^2}z_i$ the correlation is $\lambda^2$, which motivates the square root of the fitted mean correlation; flooring it at zero is a modeling choice.

Funding and premiums are resampled jointly in stationary blocks with a mean of 168 hours, which keeps their observed floor, tails and dependence across coins, and events use 24-hour blocks. What establishes the absence of edge is that these inputs are exogenous to fresh price noise, not the block lengths. Histories with fewer than 1{,}000 traded bars take median parameters, a chosen fallback for stability. The pilot's planted markets put the drift on two of twelve coins, with a funding scale of $10^{-4}$ about an interest floor of $1.25\cdot10^{-5}$ and an oracle weight of 0.1, chosen to make sparse alternatives that can be discovered. Clipping $X=\sigma x+\beta g$ at $\pm a$ changes its expectation by $\E[(-a-X)_+]-\E[(X-a)_+]$, so a tail probability alone does not bound the bias, and the planted strength $\beta$ is calibrated through the kernel on at least 200 simulated years, targeting net Sharpe. A 99\% positive-mean interval on 200 fresh years is a chosen proxy for truth when false discoveries are counted. The diagnostic probes (200 simulated years, $|z|<3.7$, a regression $p>0.001$ with heteroskedasticity-robust errors, and 100-year tolerances on moments) are chosen stochastic checks, which prove nothing for every strategy. Per-step returns avoid a statistic of compounded dollars, whose skew made a Gaussian $z$-test misleading.

\subsection{The pilot's scope and availability (choices)}\label{app:data}

Funding dislocations are the chosen family because settled funding, premiums and bars have a public accounting link, unlike predicted funding. The witness's threshold of $10^{-4}$, total target of 0.1 and sign reversal make a fixed fixture, not an optimized edge. The 730-day window, the universe of the twelve coins with the most volume in the preceding month, and the 180 days held out are choices of feasibility and breadth under limits of retention and compute; ranking in the preceding month and keeping delisted coins avoid selecting today's survivors, but nothing derives their affordability. The historical holdout of 60 days, with a one-month margin after the cutoff, was chosen for fast iteration of the pipeline, not for independence or useful power, and costs fitted in October rule out calling it untouched. The forward window of 91 days followed the cost table's freeze, with a larger margin after the cutoff.

The availability rule's $\delta=120$ seconds, its funding rule $\max(H+\delta,\ \text{reported time}+60\,\text{s})$, polling every 30 seconds, gaps of 35 seconds at boundaries and 60 seconds for books, and a 24-hour window separate lateness in the source from opportunities the recorder missed, and a one-second clock budget is small against the nominal slack. Neither that comparison nor a day of live measurement proves a historical availability rule. The 1\% limit on missing data, the 24-hour longest gap and the 99\% replay completeness are underived choices of feasibility, and the missing data could hold every relevant trade. A thousand store queries at boundaries and at random, and a 24-hour refetch, probe the implementation and revisions; they are not coverage theorems. Resolving the snapshot first avoids using a superseded availability rule, but it can hide an earlier value when a later correction exists, and bitemporal delivery is a fix the pilot does not have.

\subsection{Sources and licensing (choices)}\label{app:sources}

Declaring a source separately from binding it, certificates issued by a verifier, and transitive lineage are choices that keep a feed's builder from certifying it and keep absence from becoming zero: positive evidence of covered zeros is needed, because unseen events and no events are different observations. Terms require an affirmative grant or a recorded operator decision, and silence or a refusal of access is not permission. Stable upstream identifiers with validity intervals prevent ticker collisions. The text sources' mapping-precision cutoff of 0.8 was chosen after precision was measured but before any return, and a blind sample of 50 items has no target of confidence or dilution; owed are error weighted by the consuming signal, sampling uncertainty, and recall beyond the sampled forums. The text phase's 20 historical items, 72-hour recording, 30 observed items for a claimed-time rule, five-minute cadence, 60-second prospective margin and polling at no more than half the documented rate are chosen budgets for feasibility, politeness and evidence, not guarantees of historical availability.

Storing and processing licensed rows deterministically and disclosing only aggregates is a chosen reading of terms, not proof of legal correctness or of resistance to reconstruction. The first design of the permitted reports, a frozen list of report fields each with a minimum aggregation and a suite of four attack patterns, can be defeated: three reports over permitted cells recover one date's value (row~9). Until a design with a fixed, strategy-independent partition or noise under a budget is reviewed, no aggregate of such a source crosses, and no request for a vendor's permission or purchase of paid data is authorized. The tiers of signal study and executable backtest keep benchmark predictability from being claimed as trades, and their score bounds and conditional nulls must be derived for each claim, never from unsupported clipping.

\section{The arithmetic of confirmation}\label{app:confirm}

\subsection{What an exact test costs (derived)}\label{app:confirmer}

Log wealth at Kelly's fraction, for independent even bets of net edge $e$, is near a Brownian motion with drift $e^2/2$ and variance $e^2$ a bet. By the reflection formula, with $x=e\sqrt N$ and $b=\log20$, it reaches $b$ before $N$ bets with probability $\Phi(x/2-b/x)+20\,\Phi(-x/2-b/x)$, which is 0.80 at $x=2.931$, that is at $N=8.59/e^2$: 9{,}548 independent bets at a net edge of 0.03, 3{,}437 at 0.05, 1{,}343 at 0.08 and 859 at 0.10. A threshold of 20 required at the end of the test, rather than at its first crossing, asks for $11.8/e^2$, at which the stopped test's power is really 0.896. Kelly's fraction $\mu/\sigma^2$ fits under the cap $1/L$ when $L\le\sqrt{2190}\,\sigma/S$, that is, when a step's worst loss is at most $\sqrt{2190}/S$ of its standard deviations: 33.4 at Sharpe 1.4, 23.4 at 2 and 19.5 at 2.4. The bound is each step's own. Against the null ``mean at most zero, this step's loss at most $L_t$, known at its start'', the exact wealths are those betting a fraction of at most $1/L_t$ that step; for a long-only account $L_t$ is what it has at risk, so the largest exact bet keeps the wealth fully staked and no more.

\subsection{A forward account in bounded bets (derived; no study uses it)}\label{app:thesis-events}

\emph{The account.} Cash starts at 1. A purchase pays a stake registered by the agent, at most 5\% of cash, at the all-in cost $c<1$ of its fill, and holds $\text{stake}/c$ units. A recorded settlement $x\in[0,1]$ pays $(\text{stake}/c)\,x$ into cash. The null is rejected when cash reaches 20, and a payout not yet paid is never cash. The null is that for every purchase $\E[x\mid\mathcal G]\le c$, where $\mathcal G$ is everything that has happened by its fill, recorded or not, and the cost is known at the fill. Let $V$ be cash plus, for every position not yet recorded as paid, $\text{stake}/c$ times the conditional expectation of $x$, on the world's filtration, to which the recorder's own delays are adapted. A fill lowers cash by the stake and adds at most the stake to the sum, by the null. Between events each term is a martingale. A recording moves a realized payout from the sum into cash and changes nothing. So $V$ is a non-negative supermartingale from 1, cash never exceeds it, and cash reaches 20 with probability at most 5\%, whenever and in whatever order records arrive. Dependence between bets costs power and never validity. A bracket on a perpetual future is such a purchase only on isolated margin equal to its stake, with funding deducted.

\emph{Its power.} Where the cap does not bind, bets a week times $e^2$ must be at least $8.59/104=0.083$ for 80\% power within two years. As a check of size, an account staking the cap on fair bets, 33 a week, reaches 20 within two years in 2.4\% of simulated paths, against the bound of 5\% over any horizon.

\section{Study 1}\label{app:study1}

\subsection{Twins}

A pair is a planted market and its twin at strength zero. The generator seeds every draw from the market's identifier, and the strength changes no draw, so twins share funding, premiums, innovations and volume, and their unplanted coins are identical. They differ in the planted coins' returns and, under GARCH, in those coins' variance paths, which feed on the planted return. Twins are therefore not additive: a twin is the same world without the plant, not the planted world minus a term. A program's effect on a pair is its score on the planted market minus its score on the twin, so it holds what the plant does to the program and nothing else. Each market's pairs derive from its own identifier, so nothing random is shared between two markets' classifications, and a session never sees its fresh pairs, so a pick fitted to its own market's noise carries nothing over.

\subsection{The majority rule}

Let $q$ be a pick's chance of beating half the reference's effect on a fresh pair, the reference's effect being positive. The rule classes a pick as earning from the plant when it wins at least 39 of 57 pairs. It is the smallest rule that classes a pick at $q=1/2$ as earning with probability at most 0.005 and a pick at $q=3/4$ with probability at least 0.9: $P(\mathrm{Bin}(57,1/2)\ge39)=0.0038$ and $P(\mathrm{Bin}(57,3/4)\ge39)=0.901$, whatever the pick's variance or tails. Between the two the step is soft: 0.12 at $q=0.6$, 0.48 at 0.671 and 0.99 at 0.8. Both arms are classed by the one rule and G100's rate is calibrated under it, so a pick in the soft zone costs its arm some rate: softness can cost the agent the claim but never give it. With effects exactly opposite, a program and its mirror win disjoint pairs, since $2\cdot39>57$.

The plant's effect on a score has three parts, since a score is a mean return over its standard deviation and the plant moves both. The drift captured changes sign with every position and keeps its sign across pairs, and it is what the claim names. The plant's change in volatility, which under GARCH rescales the whole score, also changes sign with every position; for a program blind to the drift its sign on a pair is set by the program's own score on the twin, a fresh coin on every pair, and the rule bounds it at 0.0038 for any program. The even part, what the plant does to a program and its mirror alike (fees against a changed volatility, liquidation, ruin), can keep one sign across pairs, so the rule cannot bound it, and the mirror-pair check measures it.

\subsection{The sequential test}

A discordant market is the agent's win with probability at most one half under the null, whatever the joint law of the arms. Making an outcome better never undoes a claim, and removing concordant markets never does, so the worst null has every market discordant at one half, and its size, 0.043, is enumerated exactly. A discordant win adds $\log(0.7/0.5)=0.3365$ and a loss $\log(0.3/0.5)=-0.5108$ to the sum, against boundaries of $\pm\log19=\pm2.9444$: nine straight wins claim, and six straight losses stop. A session that fails is the agent's miss, and a miss in place of any outcome never makes a claim. Reruns would not be safe: a search that is losing runs longer and is cut off more often, and if each rerun hid a loss, the size would be 0.073 at one rerun, 0.117 at two and 0.338 at five. The study may end early, for any reason, as undecided at its last judged market, and a stopped study may resume at its next market under the same manifest; the size holds, since a claim is made only at the upper boundary within the first 100 markets and ending sooner only removes paths to it.

\subsection{The strength and its band}

G100's rate is the calibrated target, 0.15. A bisection on one set of 1{,}600 markets, the same markets at every step, ends when the rate is within 0.005 of 0.15, in at most 12 steps. The check runs G100 at that strength on 10{,}000 fresh markets and requires a rate between 0.12 and 0.18. Its standard error at 0.19 is 0.0039, so the true rate lies between 0.11 and 0.19 unless the estimate is off by 2.5 standard errors. An earlier design that searched on 400 markets and checked on 2{,}000 within 0.02 would stop falsely with probability 0.31; these sizes give 0.0048.

\subsection{Checks before any session}

The mirror pairs are constant long and short at the highest leverage the language allows, momentum and reversal, and a position blind to the plant at three leverages. The blind position is long the coins whose week of volume is above the cross-section's mean and short the others. Volume is drawn apart from funding, returns and the plant, and twins share it, so the position is the same on a market and its twin; and it turns over, so that fees can show. On a pair, a mirror pair's even part is the mean of its two members' effects, and it is classed as if it were a pick on 200 calibration markets. Each mirror pair may earn on at most 4 of its 200: under the rule's bound of 0.0038 a market, some pair exceeding that has probability 0.005. More means the score pays fees, liquidation or ruin on most pairs, and the measure is wrong. Constant long and constant short read nothing, so an agent could submit one with no search; each must earn on at most 14 of the 200 markets. A constant that earns as often as G100's lowest rate, 0.11, passes with probability at most 0.039, and an agent that submits a constant earning less often than G100 is under the null. A constant earning at 0.03 passes with probability 0.999, at 0.05 with 0.92 and at 0.07 with 0.57.

\subsection{The bar's certificate}

The best mix's wealth is a maximum over mixes of a product over every price event, and the kernel trusts no optimizer and keeps no history. Since $\log(1+x)\le x-x^2/2+x^3/3$ for every $x>-1$, a mix with weights $b_c$ has log wealth in a span of at most $\sum_c b_cS_{1,c}-b_c^2Q_c$, where $Q_c=S_{2,c}/2-\max(S_{3,c},0)/3$ and $S_{1,c},S_{2,c},S_{3,c}$ are the sums of coin $c$'s returns, squares and cubes over its price events. For any multiplier $\mu\ge0$, the largest of that over mixes is at most $D(\mu)=\mu+\sum_cm_c$, where $m_c$ is the largest of $(S_{1,c}-\mu)b-Q_cb^2$ over $b\in[0,1]$. $D$ is convex and piecewise quadratic, so its least lies among finitely many rationals, and Lean computes it exactly for each day and span. The bound is $(1-D/n)^{-n}\ge e^D$, with $n$ the product of $2^{40}$ and the least power of two at or above $D$, so that $D<n$ always and the bound's log is within $D/2^{40}$ of $D$. A span's bar is the greater of that bound and the most that 1 put into one of the span's coins at its first price became, its funding paid in cash; $\bar B$ is the product over spans. The bound holds at any multiplier, so validity does not rest on the least being found. It needs every return to be at least $-1$, and on a record with one below, nothing is confirmed. Its slack is about a quarter of each return's fourth power: 0.003 in log on simulated books, 0.036 with seventeen crashes of 30\%, and large only at returns no book has, where one return of 2 gives a bar of 14.4 against a best mix of 3. One misprint far from the book, five times the price and back, puts the bar out of reach for the rest of its span (9.3 million, against the best mix's 1.8), which can only fail to confirm.

\subsection{The guard}

Each of the guard's rules is a rule of the ledger's own kind: never short, paying for what it holds, trading at prices, and using nothing. Under the second rung's null the ratio of any one such rule to the null's mix is a supermartingale, and the bar is at least that mix, so a rule fixed beforehand reaches $x$ times the bar with chance at most $1/x$; over 212 days it reaches 2.39 times with chance at most 0.42, and the best in hindsight more often. The refusal is therefore a rule fixed in advance, not a test at a level. A wrong refusal costs a rung whose power is low and never the size of anything; a wrong registration costs a claim about the market. Lean computes each rule from the engine's own record of prices and funding, and nothing searches, since a search that looked less hard would refuse less. Each rule is carried in the test ledger's books: cash and whole lots, traded back to its mix at its clock's times at prices and at no cost, with each funding payment taken from its cash on the units held into it, and ended by the first payment its cash cannot meet. It keeps 1/25 in cash because the venue caps an hour's funding at 4\% of notional and a rule that kept none would end at its first charge. A quiet guard proves nothing: on a market of two coins whose moves straddle every minute's end, all four clocks see the coins move together and no mix comes near the bar, while a mix traded between the moves gains 1.0119 of the bar a minute.

\subsection{The stake and its forecast}

$\varphi$ is one of 1/16, 1/8, 1/4, 1/2 and 1, and $d$ one of six depths: the notional, at the replica's constants, of the first buy alone and of the first buy with its first one to five levels, about \$100, \$198, \$384, \$745, \$1{,}439 and \$2{,}763. The replay before the freeze runs the replica over the archive from 3 April to 31 October 2026, 212 days, holes in the record included. A block bootstrap then forecasts power for each pair: 10{,}000 paths of 730 days, each 53 blocks of 14 consecutive replay days (the last cut to 2), whose starts are fixed by a public hash of the path's and block's numbers, so no seed is registered and the paths are the same for every pair. Fourteen days is a choice, longer than the vault's longest cycle of 10.6 days; blocks of 7 and 28 days are reported beside. A path's wealth at the bid is its wealth at prices times that day's ratio of the two on the record, so on a path, as on the record, it never exceeds the wealth at prices. The chosen pair has the greatest share of paths reaching the top registered rung, with ties broken by the wealth at the bid at the replay's end, then the smaller $\varphi$, then the smaller $d$. Every pair is valid, so the choice moves power and never size.
