\title{What Can Be Confirmed About an Agent's Edge?\\[0.3em]\large Auditable search, a confirmation wall, and two positive controls}
\author{Kevin Lei}
\date{Working paper, November 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Language-model agents can search large hypothesis spaces cheaply. In mathematics that search yields results because a formal checker rejects every candidate that does not follow from its definitions. In trading research the checker is a backtest, which is noisy, non-stationary and exploitable by selection. This paper asks what can and cannot be confirmed about an agent's trading edge, and builds a system to study it. Agents write strategies in a typed language that a small trusted kernel evaluates on point-in-time data; an append-only ledger records every evaluation and every result disclosed to an agent; data sources bind only under certificates; and a statistical gate separates selection on a search period from one test on untouched data, with the whole adaptive procedure calibrated on simulated markets. The language's core invariants are proved in Lean, and a second kernel, an executable Lean engine, proves its laws about the code that runs. A pilot on Hyperliquid perpetual futures ran the system end to end. It promoted nothing under the null and nothing from planted edges of annualized Sharpe 1 to 3. The cause is arithmetic: a strategy a language model proposes can be judged only on data after the model's knowledge cutoff, so the judged span is months. At 91 days, 80\% power needs a Sharpe ratio of 5.0 for a test of fixed length, 5.9 for an exact sequential test and 27.6 for the pilot's gate; at two years the exact test needs 2.07. We call this the confirmation wall, and design the program that follows around it as two positive controls. Study~1, now running, asks whether the agent finds a planted edge more often than a genetic search of 100 evaluations, by a sequential sign test of size at most 0.043. Study~2, in construction, asks whether an exact confirmer can confirm a published rule with a live record: a long-only dip-buying vault, replicated in the Lean engine and judged over two forward years. For a long-only rule full investment is the wall, so its expected end is ``not confirmed'', and its forward run is kept as a measurement. Formal methods can make certain errors impossible; they cannot turn short evidence into confirmation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Agentic mathematics is search with a learned heuristic. Many agents propose candidate lemmas, proof steps or constructions at high throughput, and the language model plays the part of the heuristic in classical tree search: it decides which branches look promising, not which are correct. Correctness comes from elsewhere. A proof assistant such as Lean has a small trusted kernel that checks each candidate against the formal definitions, cheaply and mechanically, so wrong guesses cost almost nothing and the heuristic may be wild. Even that guarantee is narrower than it looks: the kernel certifies that a proof matches its formal statement, not that the statement captures the question. A trading evaluator has the same weakness in a stronger form, since it can compute a perfectly correct result for an incorrectly modeled market.

This paper asks how much of that structure carries over to quantitative research, and what a verifier can honestly confirm there. Three questions organize it. \emph{Capability:} when an edge exists, does an agent find it more often than cheap search? \emph{Edge:} does anything an agent proposes earn a positive expected net return, in a way that can be confirmed? \emph{Information:} does reading change an agent's forecasts for the better? The answers come in four parts.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A system for auditable search} (Section~\ref{sec:system}). Agents propose freely, and results come only through a small trusted kernel. It type-checks strategies written in a restricted language, evaluates them on point-in-time data with explicit timing and costs, and records every evaluation and every disclosure in an append-only ledger. A statistical gate separates selection, which may use anything in a search period, from inference, one test on untouched data, and the whole adaptive procedure is calibrated on simulated markets. The system is one of the project's products, not only a means to a result.
  \item \textbf{A pilot} (Section~\ref{sec:pilot}) ran the system on Hyperliquid's perpetual futures with a frontier model as the agent. It promoted nothing under the null and nothing from planted edges, and the agent's one real campaign froze no finalist.
  \item \textbf{The confirmation wall} (Section~\ref{sec:wall}). The pilot's blindness is arithmetic. A strategy a language model proposes can be judged only on data after the model's knowledge cutoff, so the judged span is months. We give the threshold at matched power for three tests, separate the part that is the calendar from the part that is the gate's design, and state the three ways the wall moves.
  \item \textbf{Two positive controls} (Section~\ref{sec:program}), designed around the wall. Study~1 asks the capability question on synthetic markets, where observations are free, and is running. Study~2 asks whether an exact confirmer confirms a rule that plausibly has an edge, a published strategy with a live record replicated and judged forward, and is in construction. We give their designs and their pre-registered criteria; neither has a result yet.
\end{enumerate}
The project is academic. It trades nowhere, reads only public data under source certificates, and claims no edge for any strategy. Every criterion that judges a number is registered before the evidence it judges exists. Section~\ref{sec:related} relates the work to the literature, and Section~\ref{sec:limits} states its limits.

\section{Search against a noisy verifier}\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Selection is not confirmation}\label{sec:selection}

The analogy to mathematics breaks at the verifier. A proof checker is exact and cannot be fooled. A backtest is a noisy estimate on non-stationary data, and searching harder against it produces better-looking results whether or not any edge exists. A pass/fail filter does not avoid this: accepting only strategies above a Sharpe threshold is still selection. In an illustrative Gaussian example, searching a thousand null strategies and ten modest real edges accepts mostly noise (Appendix~\ref{app:noise}). Agents make this worse in three ways.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Hidden trials.} An agent that tweaks a parameter, looks at the result and tweaks again has run many tests while submitting one.
  \item \textbf{Unclear effective $N$.} A thousand near-duplicates are neither 1{,}000 independent tests nor one.
  \item \textbf{Non-stationarity.} Even a correctly validated edge shows only that the edge existed in the sample period.
\end{enumerate}
Two design goals follow. The complete history of what agents saw and decided must be auditable, so that the selection procedure can be evaluated as a whole rather than one test at a time. And inference must use only data that played no part in selection.

Short histories leave little search budget. Breadth is the second budget. Grinold's fundamental law \cite{grinold1989} says that the information ratio is roughly skill per independent bet times the square root of independent bets per period. A dozen co-moving crypto assets offer few independent bets; a larger universe may offer more, but breadth must be measured from the covariance of strategy returns, not counted as instruments. Time lowers the noise ceiling, breadth raises the signal of a real edge, and trials raise the ceiling, so a search is affordable only where the first two are large. For a strategy that a language model proposes there is a third condition: only time after the model's knowledge cutoff counts (Section~\ref{sec:wall}).

\section{A system for auditable search}\label{sec:system}

\subsection{A small trusted kernel}\label{sec:arch}

The system splits into an untrusted generator and a trusted kernel, and the only way to obtain a backtest result is through the kernel (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). Agents propose strategies, each with a stated mechanism. The kernel type-checks them, reduces them to a canonical form and logs them, evaluates them, and gates them. Every result shown to an agent, including for rejected candidates, is recorded in the ledger, because it can shape what the agent tries next.

The kernel's source should be small, but the trusted computing base is larger than the kernel. Its correctness also depends on the reconstruction and ingestion of historical data, revisions and timestamps included; on source certificates and the tests that produce them (Section~\ref{sec:sources}); on the language's compiler and runtime, and the proof checker that verifies them (Section~\ref{sec:verified}); on the market simulator with its fee and execution models; and on the statistical implementations and the ledger's storage. Each needs stated invariants and tests.

``Results come only through the kernel'' is a deployment requirement, not a property of the design. An agent that can load a file of prices and run its own backtest can do unlogged research, and nothing in the kernel would know. The deployment therefore states a threat model: the channels through which an agent could learn about data or results (files, network access, tools, error messages, caches, other agents and earlier sessions) and the control that closes each. Tool permissions alone do not close these channels, so the operating system enforces them. In the pilot a research agent runs in a sandbox whose only tool is a client for a daemon. The daemon fixes the data store, enforces the evaluation budget and discloses a fixed set of fields. The client is read-only and ignores code the agent writes, the sandbox sees only the software it runs, and its only network path is a logged relay to the model's API.

For licensed data the daemon is also the licensing boundary. Some vendors' terms permit storage and deterministic processing but restrict use with language-model systems. Such a source's rows stay behind the daemon, and agents, hosted models included, receive only aggregate reports, so the terms are met at one enforced point. Our first design of those reports was shown to leak individual values (Appendix~\ref{app:defects}, row~9), so until it is redesigned no aggregate of such a source crosses. Even with every channel closed, the ledger is an audit mechanism, not a measure of information leakage. It records what was disclosed, not how much an agent inferred from it, and it cannot see what the agent already knew from training.

\subsection{A typed strategy language}\label{sec:dsl}

Agents do not write arbitrary backtests. They write strategies in a small domain-specific language designed to rule out specified classes of research bugs before anything runs. A strategy is data, an abstract syntax tree that the kernel type-checks and interprets, never host-language code. Each property below is stated as an invariant and tested, including against strategies written to evade it, and the core ones are proved (Section~\ref{sec:verified}).

\paragraph{Causality in data and in execution.} A strategy is a causal stream transformer, formally a Mealy machine: its output at time $t$ depends only on inputs it has received. A type signature describes only an interface; the guarantee comes from the runtime, which feeds the machine only point-in-time data and enforces four distinct times: when data became available, when the strategy decided, when its order could be submitted, and when that order could fill. A strategy that reads a candle's close at 12:00 and fills at that close is causal in its inputs but still leaks through execution, and timestamps alone cannot exclude it, since such a fill can satisfy every time ordering with equality.

\begin{lstlisting}
-- A strategy consumes point-in-time observations and emits orders
type Strategy = Mealy (Obs Event) Order

-- Each observation records when it became knowable and which revision it is
data Obs a = Obs { eventTime :: Time, knownAt :: Time, revision :: Int, value :: a }

-- The simulator, not the strategy, decides submission and fill times
data Fill = Fill { decidedAt :: Time, submittedAt :: Time
                 , filledAt :: Time, price :: Price }
\end{lstlisting}

Every event therefore carries a sequence number as well as a time, and the evaluator enforces two conditions. For every input to a decision,
\begin{equation}
  t_{\mathrm{known}} \le t_{\mathrm{decision}} \le t_{\mathrm{submission}} \le t_{\mathrm{fill}},
\end{equation}
and the fill is computed only from market events strictly after the order became eligible to execute, ordered by time and then by sequence number, so that events sharing a timestamp still have a definite order. Any exception, such as an auction that crosses at a price set after submission, is modeled explicitly. In the pilot, a decision using the close at $B$ occurs at $B+\delta$ and fills at the opening reference at $B+4$ hours (Figure~\ref{fig:fill}; Appendix~\ref{app:execution}). Even a correct fill rule cannot establish that a fill would have happened: queue position, cancellations, competing orders and latency are not fully visible in historical data, so fills are an estimate from an execution model. Event-driven engines such as QuantConnect's LEAN \cite{lean} already separate a time frontier from fill models, and the language must at least match that.

\paragraph{Units, dimensions and time bases.} Prices, sizes, funding rates and volatilities carry types, so adding a price to a funding rate fails to type-check. Rates also carry their time basis: an hourly and an annualized funding rate are both dimensionless ratios, but combining them without an explicit conversion is a type error. Types do not stop a feature from being normalized with future data; that is the runtime's job.

\paragraph{Levels and changes.} Two independent trending series routinely show large, meaningless correlations \cite{granger1974}. The design therefore distinguishes levels from changes: correlation, regression and ranking operators are to accept only changes, ratios or other transforms declared stationary, unless the strategy invokes a cointegration test whose result is a logged finding rather than a license. This is not built. The pilot's language has no correlation or regression operator, and its ranking operator accepts any dimensionless value.

\paragraph{Composition.} Causal transformers compose: features feed signals, signals feed sizing, sizing feeds risk limits. Laws of composition give the canonicalizer of Section~\ref{sec:ledger} rewrites to apply. Each rewrite must preserve full trading semantics, including state, order timing and fees, and in binary64 arithmetic only identities exact bit for bit qualify (Appendix~\ref{app:rewrites}).

\paragraph{Execution realism.} Fees, funding payments, slippage and latency are part of the evaluator's semantics, not optional parameters. Each venue needs its own model: on Hyperliquid, the fee tier a small account actually pays, funding schedules and order-book depth. The pilot fits one constant cost per coin, side and size bucket to a 24-hour recording of order books, so depth at the decision time is not modeled (Appendix~\ref{app:costs}). Such models are only as good as their calibration, so they are treated as validated components with stated error bounds, and where calibration data is thin the limitation is reported beside every result that depends on it.

\subsection{Sources: declaration, certification, point in time}\label{sec:sources}

A strategy should be able to say ``this depends on a feed of shipping disruptions'' before anyone has built that feed. The language therefore treats a data source as a typed declaration, separate from any concrete feed: an entity key, typed fields with units, a cadence or event semantics, and an availability policy. The known time of every observation is assigned by the recorder or the certified backfill, never by the feed itself.

\begin{lstlisting}
source ShippingAlerts : EventStream {
  key      : Region
  kind     : Enum [Closure, Delay, Attack]
  count    : Count
}  -- knownAt is assigned by the recorder, never by the feed

strategy S (alerts : ShippingAlerts, brent : Price Commodity) = ...

bind ShippingAlerts := feed "alerts-v3"   -- refused without a valid certificate
\end{lstlisting}

The type checker checks a strategy against the declared schema without any binding. Evaluation requires every source the strategy names to be bound to a certified feed; an unbound source makes evaluation undefined, not empty. Agents can therefore propose hypotheses about data that does not yet exist, and those hypotheses wait in the ledger until a feed is certified. An unbound declaration prevents fitting through this system before the feed binds, but it does not make the strategy a pre-registered prediction: the agent may already know, from training data, published research or an equivalent feed, how the relationship behaved. Only a frozen strategy evaluated on data independent of its selection supplies independent evidence.

\paragraph{Certification.} A feed binds to a declaration only under a certificate recorded in the ledger. The certificate holds the results of tests whose thresholds are fixed before the feed's data is examined against any returns.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Terms:} the certificate quotes the clause that permits the automated use, with its location and retrieval date, or shows that the feed is an official API whose terms allow it. Silence is not permission. A source whose terms forbid automated access is never fetched, and anything collected from it before that was found is deleted. Where the terms restrict the operator rather than the access, as some venues' interface terms restrict residents of certain jurisdictions, reading the public data is the operator's decision, recorded with the clauses found.
  \item \textbf{Availability:} every historical timestamp is classified as observed (recorded live, or witnessed by an independent archive capture or a protocol-enforced time), reconstructed from the source's own release records, or estimated. A lag measured on a live recording validates the availability rule going forward; applied to history it is an assumption, since lags change. A historical row's known time is therefore the later of the claimed-time rule, used only where the claimed time is protocol-enforced or server-set and the live test passed, and any independent witness. A row with neither is usable only prospectively.
  \item \textbf{Revisions:} a later re-fetch of the same history, compared against what was recorded live, so that a backtest never sees corrected values nobody had at the time.
  \item \textbf{Entity mapping:} the precision and recall of the mapping from raw records to schema keys, measured on blind-labelled samples.
  \item \textbf{Coverage:} gaps, outages, and the period over which the feed is complete.
  \item \textbf{Extractor validation} for feeds derived by a language model (below).
\end{itemize}
Agents may build feeds, but the kernel's certification tests decide whether a feed binds, so an agent never grades its own data.

\paragraph{The point-in-time store.} A known time is necessary but not sufficient: real data is revised, backfilled, delayed by ingestion and sometimes mistimestamped. The store keeps immutable revisions with lineage. A query ``as of time $t$'' resolves each key to the latest revision fetched by the dataset version and shows it only if its known time is at most $t$. It never falls back to an older revision, so a later correction hides a value rather than replacing it with one nobody had, and the revisions test measures what that costs. A revision stores only what was observed: the values, the source's own timestamps, and when it was fetched. Its known time is computed by the availability rule that the dataset version pins, so a corrected rule reaches every revision at once. Some public archives already work this way: ALFRED \cite{alfred} serves U.S.\ macroeconomic series as each vintage was published, dated by day, so it establishes which value was current on a date but not when it became tradable within that day.

\paragraph{Sources extracted by a language model.} When a language model turns raw text into structured events, it is restricted to factual fields: who, what kind of event, and when. Judgments such as sentiment or importance are excluded, because a model that knows how events turned out can shade them with hindsight. The extractor is validated with names and dates masked, its outputs are cached with the model version and prompt hash, and that version is part of every experiment's identity. Feed design choices, such as which keywords define an event, are themselves forking paths, so a feed's specification is registered before any return conditioned on it is examined.

\paragraph{Acquisition.} Free and official sources come first, and adding a feed is a declaration, a terms record and a certificate, never a code change. Data read from a blockchain's own node or official archive carries no data vendor's terms, though the access route has terms and costs of its own. Where a source keeps no past versions, our own recording is its only point-in-time record, so recording starts early and is kept in more than one copy. A schema with many sources also makes it easy to generate plausible paths from one source to another, from a central bank's statement to the currencies it affects to the commodities they price, and each such path is one more trial (Section~\ref{sec:budget}).

\subsection{The experiment ledger}\label{sec:ledger}

The ledger makes the research process auditable; it does not by itself produce a trial count that can be plugged into a correction. A canonical strategy is a program, while a statistical trial is about information: what an agent saw, and how that shaped what it selected next. An agent that inspects one strategy's drawdown, yearly returns and cost sensitivity and then proposes something new has spent multiplicity without creating any new canonical form. Conversely, rerunning an identical deterministic backtest, or rejecting a strategy at the type checker before it touches data, spends none. The ledger's storage rejects every update and deletion, and it records six things, kept distinct:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Strategy identity:} the canonical form, so that equivalent programs are recognized.
  \item \textbf{Experiment identity:} strategy, parameters, universe, data window, dataset and certificate versions, availability conventions, execution and fee model versions, extractor versions, simulator version and random seeds.
  \item \textbf{Disclosures:} every statistic returned to an agent, including for rejected candidates.
  \item \textbf{Selection decisions:} which candidates an agent or the gate chose to pursue, and what it had seen at that point.
  \item \textbf{Source certificates:} the tests and thresholds under which each feed was bound.
  \item \textbf{Registered forward predictions,} with the documents read before each.
\end{enumerate}
A result is committed to the ledger before anything about it is shown, so a failed write cannot leave unlogged research. Multiplicity is then estimated from this history, and the estimates are checked by running the whole procedure on markets with no edge (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).

\paragraph{Canonical forms.} Strategies are rewritten with the language's algebraic laws, for example commuting independent features or folding constant transforms. Equality saturation with e-graphs \cite{willsey2021} finds expressions provably equal under the supplied rules and extracts one by a cost model. It does not produce a unique form for every semantically equivalent program, and its results depend on rule coverage and resource limits. The claim is therefore narrow: the system detects some provable equivalences, conditional on the soundness of its rules, which Section~\ref{sec:verified} proves.

\paragraph{Effective $N$.} Strategies that differ syntactically can still behave almost identically. Clustering return streams by correlation gives one estimate of the effective number of independent trials, in the spirit of Bailey and L\'opez de Prado, but it is a heuristic: the right adjustment depends on the joint distribution of the test statistics, and the correlations are estimated on the sample being searched. The gate therefore counts trials raw, reports how its decisions change at the cluster counts, and treats a promotion that holds only under a favorable count as fragile.

\paragraph{Search guidance.} Agents can ask which regions of strategy space are already explored, which steers them toward new hypotheses. Telling agents where past results were good or bad is a disclosure, logged and accounted for like any other.

\subsection{The statistical gate}\label{sec:gate}

Individually valid tests do not make a valid combined procedure. The gate therefore separates selection from inference. Everything computed on the search period, the screen, Hansen's SPA, the Romano--Wolf stepdown and the mechanism check, is a selection rule: it decides which candidates reach the holdout, and its behavior is calibrated by simulation, but it supports no error claim of its own. Romano--Wolf's guarantees, for example, do not survive applying it to a family that was itself chosen on the same data. The only inference is the holdout batch: finalists frozen without any holdout data are tested once on that untouched period, with Holm's correction across them. Each campaign runs a gate fixed before it starts, and its error rate is claimed only for its finalist family on its holdout, under the holdout test's scope assumption that every net return before the venue absorbs a shortfall is at least $-2$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Search period only.} Agents see results from a research period. The holdout is never shown to them.
  \item \textbf{Screen.} A candidate must reach a deflated Sharpe ratio of 0.95, with $N$ the count of distinct canonical strategies evaluated that filled at least once (Appendix~\ref{app:dsr}). Recomputed at the counts of correlation clusters at $|\rho|\ge0.9$ and $0.5$, the screen is reported as a diagnostic of fragility. (Known defect, Table~\ref{tab:defects}, row~1.)
  \item \textbf{Global screen.} Hansen's SPA runs over every strategy evaluated in the campaign against a flat position. If it does not reject at 5\%, the campaign promotes nothing, which under the complete null stops most campaigns before any holdout is spent. (Row~1.)
  \item \textbf{Ranking.} The Romano--Wolf stepdown at 5\% runs over the screened candidates, and those it rejects become finalists. Its $p$-values serve selection only, since the family was chosen on the same data. (Row~1.)
  \item \textbf{Mechanism check.} A candidate whose pre-registered mechanism prediction fails is removed from the finalists. There is no override by a higher statistical threshold. (Row~5.)
  \item \textbf{Freeze.} The finalists, the promotion rule and the correction across finalists are fixed before the holdout is opened.
  \item \textbf{Holdout batch.} All finalists are evaluated together, once. The kernel enforces this: it reads the whole batch before evaluating any of it, accepts it only if its trials are exactly the finalists frozen in the ledger, and claims the window under a lock before the first evaluation. A finalist is promoted only if its net holdout return is positive and its holdout $p$-value passes 5\% after Holm's correction, and a promotion is then confirmed on a later forward window. (Row~2.)
  \item \textbf{No recycling.} A strategy modified after its holdout result is not retested on that holdout. A holdout used by one campaign is spent: the next campaign evaluates on a later period, and the old holdout becomes part of its search data.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The holdout test.} For each finalist $i$, the null hypothesis is that at every step of the holdout its net return, before the venue absorbs any shortfall, has non-positive mean given everything before that step, which includes everything the search revealed:
\begin{equation}
  H_{0,i}:\ \E\big[r^{\mathrm{net}}_{i,t} \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big] \le 0 \quad \text{for every } t, \qquad \mathcal{F}_{t-1} \supseteq \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{search}} .
\end{equation}
The test bets: it stakes a predictable, capped plug-in Kelly fraction of its wealth on each step's return and takes the inverse of the wealth's running maximum as a $p$-value \cite{ville1939,shafer2021}. Under a lower bound of $-2$ on pre-absorption returns this is valid under any dependence and any stopping, and Holm's correction then bounds any false promotion in the frozen family at 5\%, bankrupt paths included (Appendix~\ref{app:bet}). That bound is proved only for the corrected synthetic construction (Appendix~\ref{app:loss}), not for the frozen pilot's engine or for real markets, and the reported floor of $-1$ is not a substitute. A locked suite of null series checks the implementation, not the theorem's premises in every market. A rejection certifies a positive conditional mean at some step, not a positive unconditional mean of the unscaled strategy. (Rows~3 and~6.) The cap on the bet follows from the loss bound and costs power (Section~\ref{sec:wall}): at the pilot's holdout lengths zero promotions is not evidence of no edge.

\paragraph{Calibration and scale.} Cheap baseline searchers supply large samples of campaigns under the null and under planted edges. Expensive agent campaigns are smoke tests, with much weaker bounds (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}).

\paragraph{Across campaigns.} Error control is per campaign, and every promotion is reported beside the number of campaigns run. A project-wide claim needs a summable error budget fixed in advance (Appendix~\ref{app:bet}); alpha-investing \cite{foster2008} controls a different quantity and is not a substitute.

\paragraph{Reported, not judged.} Beside each finalist's judged result the gate reports its holdout return at twice the modeled costs, and the share of the holdout whose market conditions fall within the range the cost model was calibrated on. These appear only after the promotion decision and never choose among finalists. They exist because cost models calibrated on short windows may understate costs exactly when markets move and strategies trade most.

The deflated Sharpe ratio is a selection heuristic, not inference after adaptive search (Appendix~\ref{app:dsr}). Every answer from a holdout leaks some information, and what matters is how much each answer reveals, not only how many there are. The reusable holdout of Dwork et al.\ \cite{dwork2015} releases answers through a noisy threshold, with guarantees under adaptive queries for independent samples. The gate instead spends each holdout once, in one batch, and claims no adaptive validity beyond that.

\paragraph{Mechanism obligations.} Every strategy ships with a stated mechanism that implies tests beyond profit. It names the counterparty and why they trade at this price; they need not be wrong, since forced sellers at liquidation, hedgers paying for immediacy and index funds rebalancing on a schedule can all be acting rationally. It states conditional predictions, with tolerances, of how the effect should vary if the mechanism is real: a liquidation-cascade strategy should earn more when positions are crowded near liquidation and less when funding has normalized. And it estimates capacity and decay. Predictions are registered before they are tested and are logged like any other experiment. A failed prediction is evidence that the stated explanation is wrong, not proof that the strategy is, so it removes the candidate from that campaign's finalists, and the candidate may return with a revised mechanism in a later campaign on fresh data. Checked on the selection sample, as in the pilot, the test is weak (row~5). The working hypothesis that language models are most useful for proposing economically plausible ideas, rather than for blind search, is tested against simpler generators through the same machinery (Section~\ref{sec:study1}).

\subsection{Verified kernels}\label{sec:verified}

The language's semantics, type checker and canonicalization rules are the parts of the kernel whose correctness can be proved rather than only tested. A proof checker certifies that a proof matches its statement, not that the statement says what was intended, and an agent that writes both a law and its proof can state a weak law and prove it. Laws are therefore treated like pre-registered criteria (Section~\ref{sec:process}): written first, reviewed by a model from another lab, frozen, and never weakened. A law found wrong gets a dated revision, and proofs of the old statement do not count toward the new one.

\paragraph{The reference kernel.} The pilot's production engine is written in Rust. A Lean~4 \cite{demoura2021} model of its language and simulator serves as a reference, in which six theorems are proved.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Non-interference.} For every well-typed strategy $s$, two input histories that agree on every observation known by time $t$ produce the same decision at $t$:
  \begin{equation}
    h_1|_{\mathrm{known} \le t} = h_2|_{\mathrm{known} \le t} \implies \mathrm{decide}_s(h_1, t) = \mathrm{decide}_s(h_2, t).
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Unit soundness.} Every well-typed expression evaluates to a value of its declared unit and time basis.
  \item \textbf{Rewrite soundness.} Each canonicalization rule preserves the strategy's full trading semantics, including state and order timing.
  \item \textbf{Fill ordering.} Every fill satisfies both timing conditions of Section~\ref{sec:dsl}.
  \item \textbf{Binding.} Evaluation is undefined for any strategy that names an unbound source.
  \item \textbf{Bounded returns.} Losses are capped at account equity, so every reported per-step return is at least $-1$.
\end{enumerate}
Numbers are abstract in the proofs, since several algebraic identities, associativity of addition among them, are false in binary64. The canonicalizer therefore admits only rewrites that are exact in binary64, and the reference and the production engine are compared bit for bit, on complete node streams, over a frozen corpus of programs and markets. A second candidate reference, in another language, was held to the same criteria and removed (Section~\ref{sec:pilot-sources}).

\paragraph{What proofs reach.} Proofs establish properties of a formal semantics, the first of three levels of assurance (Table~\ref{tab:assurance}). A simulator can satisfy its fill-ordering theorem while charging the wrong fee schedule or misjudging liquidity, and two implementations that agree can share one wrong model of the market.

\paragraph{Kernel version 2.} The pilot's engine evaluates a fixed four-hour grid of bars under a constant cost table. Replaying a real account's orders needs more: limit and maker orders at any timescale, fills against recorded order books and trades, and an exact account. The second kernel is a clean-room design for that, written as an executable Lean program. Everything that decides a number is a pure Lean function: the strategy parser and checker, both evaluators, order handling, the fill rules, the ledger with its fees, funding, margin and liquidation, the reading of recorded decimals, the report, and the confirmer of study~2. OCaml carries only data loading, input and output, and orchestration. The laws are theorems about the functions that run, stated and frozen before their proofs and resting only on Lean's three standard axioms. Where a function is too slow, a faster twin is proved equal to it and substituted by the compiler, so no law speaks of code that does not run.

Four features of its semantics matter for what follows. The account is exact: prices, sizes, money and rates are rationals, and nothing in the ledger rounds. Strategy expressions stay in binary64, with IEEE~754's correctly rounded arithmetic, and the engine computes $\exp$ and $\log$ itself from exact series, returning the binary64 nearest the true value or, where its interval cannot decide, an undefined value rather than a wrong rounding; every float is therefore the same on every host. Time is a stream of recorded events (listings, funding settlements, trades, book snapshots and observations of declared sources), each with the time it happened and the time it became known; a coin with no fresh book snapshot for a registered length is stale, and nothing of it fills. Fills follow one of two named rules. The \emph{pessimistic} rule credits a resting buy only on a later recorded seller-initiated trade below its price, in that trade's size; the \emph{optimistic} rule fills it whenever the record does not exclude it. Neither bounds the other, and judged runs use the pessimistic rule. Finally, no fill beats the tape: every fill is at a price no better than the coin's price after its event, and no fee is negative, so a trade never raises an account's value at the moment it happens. Study~2's validity rests on that law (Section~\ref{sec:study2}).

Bars remain available as a derived source: a bar becomes two trades at its extremes and a one-level quote at its close, under four registered assumptions about the spread, the depth, the path and whether the extremes traded. The engine refuses any event dated in a protected window until that window's release is committed, so no report or trace can hold protected data. A run's identity hashes its program, every input file to the length read, the venue model and the content hash of the build that made the executable, and nothing is published before its result is committed to the ledger. The two kernels share one component, the float expression language, and version~2's evaluator is checked bit for bit against the pilot's Lean reference rebuilt with correctly rounded $\exp$ and $\log$ and a numerically stable rule for rolling windows. When study~1 ends the pilot's engine is retired; its judged results stay reproducible from an archived build, and none is rerun on the new one. Version~2 is being built, and the parts complete so far have their laws proved.

\subsection{Pre-registration and independent checks}\label{sec:process}

Statistical procedures fail most often where someone can choose after seeing a number. Every condition that judges a number someone could game is registered before the evidence it judges exists: measurable, with thresholds, and with each number derived from the claim it supports or stated as a choice with its reason. Before registration, reviewers try to construct a run that passes while the claim is false and one that fails while it holds. A registered criterion is never edited. A wrong one gets a dated revision, and the work is judged only on evidence produced after it. After a failure a criterion may be amended only if the change cannot increase false discoveries; the amendment is labelled as such and never reported as a pass of the original. A defect in the procedure itself that a failure exposes is fixed only as a new version judged on fresh evidence, and the failure stays recorded. Operational readiness is not pre-registered: it is a test suite.

Correctness is checked by someone other than the builder. Tests are written from the specification by a session that has not read the code, and by a model from another lab where a judged result depends on them. A defect starts as a failing test, and failure paths are tested by killing the process. A red team that has not seen the source attacks each kernel milestone, and each of its successes becomes a permanent test. Before a phase ends, a fresh session hunts for bugs in the code it rests on, and independent implementations of the statistics, written from the specification by another lab's model, must agree with ours.

Only the gate's holdout step reads a holdout, once per campaign. Nothing derived from an unreleased holdout or a reserved forward window reaches research, selection or anyone reviewing work not yet frozen. Such recordings are inspected only for integrity (their status, sizes, hashes and gaps), never for prices or returns.
